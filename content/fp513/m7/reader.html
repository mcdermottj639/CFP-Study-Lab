LO 7.1.1 · Return building blocks: simple, compound, HPR, total return, SEC yield

−

Every return measure answers “how much did I make?” with a different assumption about time and reinvestment. Know which assumption each one makes.

MeasureWhat it doesBlind spot
Simple rateTotal return ÷ years held (annualized, no compounding)Ignores the time value of money
Compound rateAssumes interest and repaid principal are reinvested; solve with TVM or IRR keysReinvestment may not really happen at that rate
Holding period return (HPR)Whole-period gain incl. income ÷ starting valueNo time element: overstates the annual figure if held > 1 year, understates if < 1 year
Total returnIncome + capital appreciation, usually for one yearMixes two components clients value differently
SEC yieldStandardized fund yield: interest + dividends over the latest 30-day period (2026 edition figure), net of expenses; disclosed in the prospectusIncome only; no price change
HPR: (ending value − beginning value ± cash flows) ÷ beginning value
HPR from periodic returns: [(1 + r1)(1 + r2)…(1 + rn)] − 1
Simple rate: total return ÷ number of years
🧮 Worked example — three ways to describe one result

Dana's account earned 45% in total over 6 years. Luis bought a bond for $960, collected $140 of interest and sold for $1,010.

  1. Dana's simple rate: 45% ÷ 6 = 7.50% per year.
  2. Dana's compound rate: PV −1, FV 1.45, N 6 → I/YR = 6.39% (lower, because growth compounds).
  3. Luis's HPR: (1,010 − 960 + 140) ÷ 960 = 190 ÷ 960 = 19.79% — but we don't know if he held it 1 year or 5.
  4. Total return on a $40 stock that pays $1.60 and is sold a year later at $43: (1.60 + 3) ÷ 40 = 11.5%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing the HPR by the ending value; leaving out interest/dividends; dividing by the wrong number of years.
Try it: Buy at $50, receive $2 of dividends, sell at $47. HPR? (47 − 50 + 2) ÷ 50 = −2.00%
Exam trap: an HPR can't be compared across investments until you know the holding period. A multi-year HPR always looks bigger than the true annual return.
Client conversation: “Your statement shows 19.8% since you bought, but that covers several years. Per year it's much less — let's look at the annual figure before comparing it with anything.” For income-focused retirees, point to the income piece of total return; for younger high-bracket earners, the growth piece usually matters more.

LO 7.1.1 · Averaging returns: arithmetic vs. geometric mean

−
  • Arithmetic mean (AM) — plain average of the periodic returns; no compounding, no reinvestment.
  • Geometric mean (GM) — the constant compound rate that turns $1 into the actual ending value; the preferred way to state a security's historical return.
AM = (r1 + r2 + … + rn) ÷ n
GM = [(1 + r1)(1 + r2)…(1 + rn)]1/n − 1
Calculator: PV = −1, FV = product of (1 + r), PMT = 0, N = n, solve I/YR.
🧮 Worked example — four years of fund returns

Returns: +10%, −5%, +18%, +7%.

  1. AM = (10 − 5 + 18 + 7) ÷ 4 = 7.50%.
  2. Product: 1.10 × 0.95 × 1.18 × 1.07 = 1.3194 (that is also the 4-year HPR of 31.94%).
  3. GM: PV −1, FV 1.3194, N 4 → 7.18%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dropping the minus sign on a losing year; dividing the cumulative return by n and calling it the geometric mean (31.94 ÷ 4 = 7.98%).
Try it: +20% then −20%. AM? GM? AM 0%; GM = (1.2 × 0.8)1/2 − 1 = −2.02% — you actually lost money.
Exam trap: GM < AM unless every period's return is identical (then they're equal). Any answer with GM above AM is wrong on sight.
Client conversation: “An average of 7.5% sounds good, but what your money actually compounded at was about 7.2%. The ups and downs cost you a little, and the compound number is the one to plan with.”

LO 7.1.1 · Time-weighted vs. dollar-weighted return

−
⚖️ Commonly confused — time-weighted vs. dollar-weighted
Time-weighted (TWR)Dollar-weighted (DWR)
Investor's later buys/sellsIgnoredIncluded
MeasuresThe investment / the managerThe investor's own experience
Cash flowsOne unit: −price, income, ending priceAll dollars in and out; final CF = value of everything held
Used forMost published mutual fund returnsThe client's personal rate (an IRR)
How to tell: if the question asks about the stock/fund, follow one share and ignore extra purchases. If it asks about the investor, net every purchase and withdrawal into the cash flows.
🧮 Worked example — Omar adds a share

Omar buys 1 share at $40. End of Year 1: $2 dividend, buys a 2nd share at $46. End of Year 2: sells both at $52; no Year-2 dividend.

  1. TWR (one share): CF0 −40, CF1 +2, CF2 +52 → IRR = 16.54%.
  2. DWR (Omar's dollars): CF0 −40, CF1 2 − 46 = −44, CF2 2 × 52 = +104 → IRR = 15.37%.
  3. Omar earned less than the stock because he put more money in just before a weaker year.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: forgetting the dividend in CF1; valuing only one share at the end of a DWR; including the extra purchase in a TWR.
Try it: Rosa invests $8,000; the fund pays her $120 cash at the end of each quarter; after the 4th dividend her shares are worth $8,300. DWR? CF0 −8,000; CF1–3 120; CF4 8,420 → quarterly IRR 2.40% × 4 = 9.62%.
Exam trap: for sub-annual cash flows the textbook annualizes the periodic IRR by multiplying (quarterly × 4), not by compounding. Compounding gives 9.97% above — not the book's answer.
Client conversation: “The fund's published number tells you how the manager did. Your number also reflects when you added or pulled money. If you invested more right before a dip, your return will trail the fund's even though you own the same thing.”

LO 7.1.1 · Inflation, taxes and compounding: real return, after-tax return, APR vs. EAR

−
  • Nominal rate — stated rate before inflation (for a bond, its coupon rate); before tax for taxable investments.
  • Real rate — inflation-adjusted rate; use the exact formula, not subtraction.
  • APR — periodic rate × periods per year; no compounding.
  • EAR — the true annual rate once intra-year compounding is counted.
Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1] × 100
After-tax return = nominal × (1 − marginal rate)  (federal + state if the state taxes it)
After-tax real = [(1 + after-tax) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1] × 100
EAR = (1 + i ÷ n)n − 1  where i = stated annual rate, n = compounding periods per year
🧮 Worked example — Priya's 7% bond

7% nominal coupon; inflation 2.5%; combined marginal tax rate 30%.

  1. Real (pre-tax): 1.07 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 4.39% (the shortcut 7 − 2.5 = 4.5% is close but not exact).
  2. After-tax: 7% × 0.70 = 4.90%.
  3. After-tax real: 1.049 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 2.34%.
  4. Card at 1.5%/month: APR = 18%; EAR = (1 + 0.18 ÷ 12)12 − 1 = 19.56%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying (1 + nominal)(1 + inflation); taxing the real return instead of the nominal return; reporting the monthly rate as the APR.
Try it: A CD pays a stated 5% compounded quarterly. EAR? (1 + 0.05 ÷ 4)4 − 1 = 5.09%.
Exam trap: the exam wants the division formula for real return. Over long projections (e.g., retirement needs) the subtraction shortcut creates sizable dollar errors.
Client conversation: “Your bond pays 7%, but after taxes and inflation you're really growing your buying power about 2.3% a year. That's the number that matters for your retirement plan.”

LO 7.2.1 · Risk-adjusted performance: Jensen's alpha, Treynor, Sharpe

−
MeasureFormulaRisk usedTypeUse when
Jensen's alpharp − [rf + (rm − rf)βp]BetaAbsoluteDiversified portfolio, R² ≥ 0.70
Treynor(rp − rf) ÷ βpBetaRelativeDiversified portfolio, R² ≥ 0.70
Sharpe(rp − rf) ÷ σpStd. deviation (total)RelativeAlmost always; required when R² < 0.70
  • Positive alpha — beat the CAPM required return; plots above the SML. Negative — below the SML.
  • Relative measure — a lone Treynor or Sharpe number means nothing; compare it with another fund or the benchmark. Higher is better.
🧮 Worked example — one portfolio, three measures

Return 11%, beta 1.2, standard deviation 16%, market 9%, risk-free 3%, R² 0.85.

  1. CAPM required: 3 + 1.2(9 − 3) = 10.2%. Alpha = 11 − 10.2 = +0.80%.
  2. Treynor = (11 − 3) ÷ 1.2 = 6.67.
  3. Sharpe = (11 − 3) ÷ 16 = 0.50.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: subtracting the market return instead of the risk-free rate; dividing by beta in Sharpe or by σ in Treynor; forgetting to subtract rf at all. Whole numbers or decimals both work if you are consistent.
Try it: Return 8%, beta 0.8, σ 10%, market 9%, rf 3%. Alpha = 8 − (3 + 0.8 × 6) = +0.20%; Treynor = 5 ÷ 0.8 = 6.25; Sharpe = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.50.
Exam trap: an undiversified fund can look great on Treynor/Jensen (which see only beta) and poor on Sharpe (which sees total risk). If R² < 0.70, ignore the beta-based answers and pick by Sharpe.
Client conversation: “This manager beat the market by 2%, but took on more risk to do it. After adjusting for that risk, he added about 0.8% — still positive, which is what we want to see.”
🔬 Go deeper — why Sharpe is the catch-all+

Beta only describes market (systematic) risk and is meaningful only when the portfolio moves with the index (high R²). Standard deviation captures all variability, so Sharpe works for single stocks, sector funds and undiversified portfolios. A crude alternative the textbook mentions: return ÷ beta (e.g., 9% ÷ 1.2 = 7.5%) compared with the index's return — again only if beta is reliable.

LO 7.2.1 · Information ratio and choosing the right measure

−

The information ratio grades an active manager: how much extra return was earned per unit of extra risk taken by straying from the benchmark.

IR = (RP − RB) ÷ σA
where RB = benchmark return (CAPM's required return may be used) and σA = standard deviation of the excess return (tracking error, “active risk”)
  • 0.50 or higher — often a top-quartile manager in its asset class.
  • Risk-free benchmark — IR reduces to the Sharpe ratio.
  • Negative alpha — IR becomes very hard to interpret; use it only when alpha is positive and beta is meaningful.
🧮 Worked example — two active managers

Benchmark 8%. Fund Q: 9.5% return, tracking error 3%. Fund R: 10% return, tracking error 6%.

  1. Q: (9.5 − 8) ÷ 3 = 0.50.
  2. R: (10 − 8) ÷ 6 = 0.33.
  3. Pick Q: less return, but far more efficient per unit of active risk.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using the fund's total standard deviation instead of tracking error; skipping the benchmark subtraction.
Try it: Fund K: 10%, σ 12%, β 0.9. Fund L: 13%, σ 20%, β 1.1. rf 3%, R² about 0.45. Which is better? R² < 0.70 → Sharpe only: K = 0.58, L = 0.50 → K (even though Treynor would favor L).
⚖️ Commonly confused — Sharpe vs. information ratio
SharpeInformation ratio
NumeratorReturn over risk-free rateReturn over benchmark
DenominatorTotal standard deviationStd. deviation of excess return
How to tell: “tracking error” or “active risk” in the question → information ratio.
Exam trap: compare Sharpe ratios only within one asset class. Recent winners cluster at the top, so ranking across classes would throw out an entire lagging class such as emerging markets.
Client conversation: “Both managers beat the index, but one took twice the off-benchmark risk to get a bit more. I'd rather pay for the one who's more efficient with the risk he takes.”

LO 7.3.1 · Market indices and benchmarks

−

Price-weighted (DJIA, 30 stocks): sum of prices ÷ a divisor now below 1.00. A $90 stock moves it 3× as much as a $30 stock.

Cap-weighted (S&P 500, Russell 2000, most indices): weight = firm value ÷ index value. Adjusts for splits and stock dividends automatically; preferred in modern portfolio theory.

  • DJIA flaws — ignores cash dividends and stock dividends under 10%, ignores shares outstanding, small blue-chip sample; still correlates above 0.95 with the S&P 500.

Whole U.S. marketWilshire 5000

Emerging marketsMSCI Emerging Markets, S&P/IFC Investable

🧮 Worked example — blended benchmark

Client: 50% U.S. large cap, 30% U.S. small cap, 20% international developed; portfolio return 7.9%. Index returns: S&P 500 10%, Russell 2000 6%, EAFE 4%.

  1. Blend = 0.5(10) + 0.3(6) + 0.2(4) = 7.6%.
  2. Portfolio 7.9% > 7.6% → it beat its true benchmark, even though it trailed the S&P 500.
  3. A $45B company in a $300B cap-weighted index drives 45 ÷ 300 = 15% of the index's moves.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: judging the whole portfolio against the S&P 500; averaging index returns without the client's weights (6.67%).
Try it: 60% S&P 500 (12%) and 40% Aggregate Bond (3%). Blended benchmark? 0.6 × 12 + 0.4 × 3 = 8.4%.
Exam trap: a small-cap fund is judged against a small-cap index (Russell 2000), not the S&P 500. A fund can lead its class and still look weak next to a hot large-cap index.
Client conversation: “The S&P was up 10%, but only half your money is in big U.S. companies. Against a yardstick built the way you're actually invested, you came out ahead.”

LO 7.4.1 · Bond yields: current yield, YTM, YTC

−
Calculator defaults (unless told otherwise): FV = +$1,000 face; PMT = $1,000 × coupon ÷ 2; N = years × 2; 2 P/Yr; END mode; price = −PV. Semiannual applies to zero-coupon bonds too. The I/YR shown with 2 P/Yr is already annual.
Current yield = annual coupon interest ÷ current market price
YTM = solve I/YR with FV = par and N = periods to maturity
YTC = same, but FV = call price and N = periods to first call
  • YTM — the bond's IRR if held to maturity; assumes coupons are reinvested at the YTM itself.
  • Reinvestment risk — if rates drop, coupons are reinvested for less and the realized return falls short of the YTM. Zeros have none.
🧮 Worked example — discount and premium bonds

Bond A: 8 years, 5% coupon, price $940. Bond B: 10 years, 7% coupon, price $1,080, callable in 4 years at $1,020.

  1. A: CY = 50 ÷ 940 = 5.32%. YTM: PV −940, FV 1,000, PMT 25, N 16 → 5.95%.
  2. B: CY = 70 ÷ 1,080 = 6.48%. YTM: PV −1,080, FV 1,000, PMT 35, N 20 → 5.93%.
  3. B: YTC: PV −1,080, FV 1,020, PMT 35, N 8 → 5.21%.
  4. Zero: 10 years, price $600: PV −600, FV 1,000, PMT 0, N 20 → 5.17%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using N = years with a semiannual PMT; reporting the semiannual rate without doubling; using par instead of the call price for YTC.
Try it: 15-year, 6% coupon, price $930, callable at par in 5 years. CY 6.45%; YTM 6.75%; YTC 7.71% → YTC > YTM > CY > coupon.
⚖️ Commonly confused — discount vs. premium ordering
BondTypical ordering
Discount (price < par)coupon < CY < YTM < YTC
Premium (price > par)coupon > CY > YTM > YTC
How to tell: a discount bond's gain to par comes sooner if called, so YTC is highest; a premium bond's loss to par comes sooner, so YTC is lowest. Exception: a premium call price well above par can lift YTC above YTM.
Exam trap: if the problem says the coupon is paid annually, switch to 1 P/Yr, PMT = full annual coupon and N = years.
Client conversation: “This bond shows a nice yield to maturity, but it's priced above face and can be called in four years. If that happens, you'd earn closer to 5.2%, so that's the number I'd count on.”

LO 7.4.1 · Taxable equivalent yield (TEY)

−

TEY converts a tax-free muni yield into the pre-tax yield a taxable bond would need to match it. Compare the TEY with the taxable bond's yield (same quality and maturity) — or, equivalently, compare after-tax yields.

Federal only (CFP Board formula): TEY = tax-exempt yield ÷ (1 − marginal rate)
Reverse: tax-free equivalent = taxable yield × (1 − marginal rate)
Double tax-exempt, itemizer: TEY = muni yield ÷ [(1 − SMTB)(1 − FMTB)] = muni yield ÷ (1 − [FMTB + SMTB(1 − FMTB)])
Double tax-exempt, non-itemizer: TEY = muni yield ÷ (1 − FMTB − SMTB)
🧮 Worked example — three investors
  1. 3.6% muni, 35% federal, no state tax: 3.6 ÷ 0.65 = 5.54%.
  2. Grace (itemizes), 3.4% in-state muni, 24% federal, 6% state: 3.4 ÷ (0.94 × 0.76) = 3.4 ÷ 0.7144 = 4.76%.
  3. Same facts but Grace doesn't itemize: 3.4 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 4.86%.
  4. Reverse: a 6% taxable bond at a 32% rate is worth 6 × 0.68 = 4.08% tax-free.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying the muni yield by (1 − t); dividing by t instead of (1 − t); comparing a pre-tax corporate yield directly with a tax-free yield.
Try it: Felix (32%, no state tax): 6% corporate or 4.3% muni? TEY 4.3 ÷ 0.68 = 6.32% > 6% → muni.
Exam trap: the Board's formula sheet uses federal tax only. Use the state version only when the question gives a state rate and says the bond is exempt from both.
Client conversation: “The muni's 4.3% looks lower, but you keep all of it. A taxable bond would have to pay about 6.3% to leave you the same amount after tax.”
🔬 Go deeper — why the itemizer formula isn't a simple sum+

For an itemizer, paying less state tax also means a smaller state-tax deduction on Schedule A, which nudges federal taxable income up. The term SMTB(1 − FMTB) captures that offset, so the combined effective rate is a little lower than the simple sum and the TEY a little lower than the non-itemizer figure.
