Case overview · how to work a case study

−

Module 9 is a practice case. Instead of new theory, it asks you to apply Modules 1–8 to one family. The Calloway case below is an original practice case with 22 linked decisions.

Case facts analysis

  • Read the whole case slowly and take notes.
  • Flag the contextual variables: age, marital status, children, net worth, income, special circumstances.
  • When a topic is raised and later expanded, expect a question on it.
  • Answer the easy questions first; park long calculations.

Question review

  1. Read the last line of each question.
  2. Make notes.
  3. Answer rule-only questions; eliminate impossible choices.
  4. Read the case for fact-specific questions.
  5. Check every question is answered.
  6. Review.
Exam trap: Case questions often include facts that belong to a different question. Before calculating, write down which 2–3 facts the question really needs (for example, T-bill interest needs the federal rate only).
Client conversation: “Before I recommend anything, I need the whole picture: your balance sheet, cash flow, taxes, goals and how you feel about risk. Every recommendation has to fit all of those at once.”
🔬 Go deeper — what the planner reviews before recommending+

Goals and time horizon, risk tolerance, tax situation, liquidity and marketability needs, and the statement of financial position and cash-flow statement. If the goals are unrealistic for the resources, revise them; fairly certain expected resources (bonus, inheritance) may be counted.

Decision 1 · LO 8.4.1 · IPS: liquidity and risk tolerance

−

The IPS sets objectives, the allocation policy, management procedures and communication. Its core content: return requirement, risk tolerance, liquidity, time horizon, laws and regulations, taxes, unique circumstances, and permitted/excluded investments.

  • Liquidity — emergency reserve and known outlays within about 3–5 years; many advisors keep that money out of stocks.
  • Ability to take risk — set by resources and circumstances (wealth, income, insurance, horizon).
  • Willingness to take risk — emotional comfort with swings; can be low even when ability is high.
  • Return requirement — must be consistent with the risk objective; if not, take more risk, lower goals, or both.
🧮 Worked solution — Q1 and Q2

Six-month reserve; Leah’s tuition in 3 years; Graham panic-sold in a past bear market; goal needs 6.5%.

  1. Reserve + tuition in 3 years = cash the clients must reach → Liquidity section. Answer (Q1): Liquidity
  2. Ability: $2.05M net worth, two incomes, insured, 12-year horizon → high.
  3. Willingness: past panic selling, fear of a 15% drop → low.
  4. Record both, then test whether the risk level they will accept can earn 6.5%; if not, discuss more risk vs. a smaller goal. Answer (Q2): High ability but low willingness; the IPS should record both and reconcile the risk level with the 6.5% requirement
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Treating the questionnaire score as the whole answer; putting near-term cash needs under “return requirement”.
Try it: If the planner found the goal needed 9% while Graham would accept only a conservative mix, what are the three ways to reconcile?
⚖️ Commonly confused — ability vs. willingness
AbilityWillingness
Driven byWealth, income, insurance, horizonFeelings, past behavior
CallowaysHighLow (Graham)
How to tell: If the fact is a number or a resource, it speaks to ability; if it is a reaction or a feeling, it speaks to willingness.
Exam trap: A rich client is not automatically an aggressive one; the IPS must address both dimensions.
Client conversation: “Your finances could ride out a rough market, but if a drop would make you sell, the plan has to account for that, or we lower the target together.”

Decision 2 · LO 8.6.1 · LO 1.3.1 · LO 7.1.1 · Reserves and crisis response

−

A reserve held in liquid, marketable form lets clients absorb setbacks without selling long-term assets or borrowing. Its yield matters less than its safety and access.

  • MMDA — bank deposit, FDIC insured up to $250,000 per ownership category (2026 edition figure), limited check-writing.
  • Money market mutual fund — investment company product, very liquid, not federally insured.
  • T-bills — federally taxed, state-exempt; the risk-free proxy.
  • Noise / incremental / discontinuous change — ignore / watch / revisit the IPS.
After-tax, inflation-adjusted return: [(1 + r × (1 − t)) ÷ (1 + i) − 1] × 100
where r = nominal yield, t = marginal tax rate that applies to that income, i = inflation
🧮 Worked solution — Q3, Q4 and Q5

Living expenses $13,500/month; liquid assets $18,000 + $24,000 + $20,000; T-bills 4.0%; 32% federal; inflation 3.0%.

  1. Target 6 × $13,500 = $81,000; liquid $62,000; gap $19,000. Answer (Q3): $19,000; add it to a money market holding from the cash-flow surplus or taxable assets
  2. MMF is not FDIC insured; an MMDA is. Answer (Q4): The mutual fund is not federally insured; a bank money market deposit account is insured up to $250,000 per ownership category
  3. T-bill after tax: 4.0% × 0.68 = 2.72% (no state tax).
  4. Real: 1.0272 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = −0.27%. Answer (Q5): −0.27%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Counting the 401(k) or a credit line as the reserve; applying state tax to T-bill interest; subtracting inflation instead of dividing.
Try it: Redo Q5 for a 4.5% bank CD (taxed by the state too) at 37% combined: 4.5% × 0.63 = 2.835%; 1.02835 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = −0.16%.
🧮 Worked solution — Q6 and Q7

Stocks fall 25%; jobs and reserve intact. Later: raise, one-week dip, Nadia’s layoff.

  1. A market drop with unchanged goals, jobs and reserves is not a reason to abandon the IPS: stay the course. Answer (Q6): Keep a long-term perspective and hold the IPS allocation, reminding Graham why it was chosen
  2. Raise = incremental; one-week dip = noise; layoff = discontinuous → revisit the IPS. Answer (Q7): III only
Exam trap: When a client insists on moving to cash during a downturn, the textbook answer is usually to maintain the allocation and the long-term view.
Client conversation: “Your reserve is there so you never have to sell stocks in a bad market. Today’s drop doesn’t change your goals; losing a job would, and then we’d rework the plan together.”
🔬 Go deeper — why time matters+

Markets show a long-term positive bias: over time the patient, diversified investor is favored. Managing the account to short-term swings tends to raise trading costs and miss the early stage of rising markets.

Decision 3 · Allocation and the Kestrel concentration (LO 8.5.2 · LO 5.2.2 · LO 8.2.1)

−

Two problems: how to structure the long-term mix, and what to do with a stock that is 24.9% of investment assets and 75% of the taxable equity sleeve.

  • Core and satellite — passive index core (strategic) plus a smaller higher-risk satellite (tactical).
  • Exchange fund — contribute concentrated stock for a share of a diversified pool, no immediate capital gains tax.
  • Protective put — own stock + buy put: floor at strike, keeps upside, costs premium.
  • Zero-cost collar — own stock + buy put + write call; call premium pays for the put; upside capped.
CAPM: ri = rf + (rm − rf)βi
If expected return < required return, the asset plots below the SML: reject.
🧮 Worked solution — Q8 and Q9

Graham wants passive and low-cost; Nadia wants an active EM/REIT sleeve. KDX $360,000, basis $160,000, value to stay in family.

  1. Passive core + active satellite fits both. Answer (Q8): A core-and-satellite strategy: a passive index core plus a smaller active satellite sleeve
  2. Diversify without immediate tax and keep the value → exchange fund (CRT gives the remainder to charity; sale taxes the $200,000 gain). Answer (Q9): Contribute the shares to an exchange fund
🧮 Worked solution — Q10 and Q11

4,000 KDX at $90; 40 puts, $80 strike, $2.50 premium.

  1. Premium = 40 × 100 × $2.50 = $10,000.
  2. Floor = 4,000 × $80 = $320,000.
  3. Net floor = $320,000 − $10,000 = $310,000. Answer (Q10): $310,000
  4. No-cost floor at $80, upside given up above $105 → buy $80 put, write $105 call. Answer (Q11): Buy 40 $80 puts and write 40 $105 calls with offsetting premiums
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Ignoring the premium ($320,000); subtracting the premium from today’s value ($350,000).
🧮 Worked solution — Q12

rf 4.0%, rm 10.0%, β 1.40, expected 11.0%.

  1. Market risk premium = 10.0% − 4.0% = 6.0%.
  2. Stock risk premium = 6.0% × 1.40 = 8.4%.
  3. Required = 4.0% + 8.4% = 12.4% > 11.0% expected → do not add. Answer (Q12): 12.4%; the expected return falls short, so do not add to KDX
⚠️ Common wrong methods: 18.0% (rf + rmβ), 8.4% (premium only), 14.0% (rmβ).
Try it: What required return would KDX have if its beta were 0.90? (4% + 6% × 0.9 = 9.4%.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — protective put vs. zero-cost collar
Protective putZero-cost collar
PositionsLong stock + long putLong stock + long put + short call
CostPremium paidAbout zero
UpsideUnlimitedCapped at call strike
How to tell: If the client will not pay premium and accepts a ceiling, it is a collar.
Exam trap: A covered call is not a hedge against a crash; it only cushions the drop by the premium received.
Client conversation: “Kestrel has been great, but a quarter of your investments ride on one company that also pays your salary. We can spread that risk without a big tax bill, and set a floor while we do it.”

Decision 4 · Risk measures and fund selection (LO 6.2.1 · LO 6.2.5 · LO 7.2.1 · LO 3.6.1)

−

Beta measures systematic risk and is meaningful only when the holding is diversified and R² is high. R² also tells you whether a fund will diversify an index holding. Fund selection adds costs, turnover, manager continuity and tax efficiency.

Weighted beta: Σ(valuei × βi) ÷ total value
R² = (correlation)²; Morningstar cutoff 0.70 (R ≈ 0.84)
Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp   Treynor = (Rp − Rf) ÷ βp
🧮 Worked solution — Q13

KDX $360,000 (β 1.40); Harborline $85,000 (1.15); Summit $37,060 (0.90).

  1. Products: $504,000 + $97,750 + $33,354 = $635,104.
  2. Total value: $482,060.
  3. $635,104 ÷ $482,060 = 1.32. Answer (Q13): 1.32
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Simple average (1.15); KDX alone (1.40).
🧮 Worked solution — Q14 and Q15

Correlations 0.94 / 0.89 / 0.86 / 0.62; Alder 11.6%, σ 16, R² 0.92; Bexley 13.0%, σ 22, R² 0.58; rf 4%.

  1. Square each R: 0.88, 0.79, 0.74, 0.38 → only 0.38 is under 0.70. Answer (Q14): International Developed Index, R² = 0.38
  2. Bexley’s R² 0.58 < 0.70, so its beta (and Treynor 7.50) is unreliable.
  3. Sharpe: Alder 7.6 ÷ 16 = 0.48; Bexley 9.0 ÷ 22 = 0.41. Answer (Q15): Alder, because its Sharpe ratio (0.48) beats Bexley’s (0.41)
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Picking Bexley on Treynor; picking on raw return.
🧮 Worked solution — Q16

Harborline: expense ratio 1.25%, turnover 140%, manager left, top-10% last year.

  1. Turnover costs (commissions, spreads, market impact) sit outside the expense ratio.
  2. High turnover can mean taxable gains distributions.
  3. Manager change → monitor closely. Answer (Q16): I, II, and IV
Exam trap: Last year’s top ranking is not a selection reason; compare funds of the same style, net of costs, on a risk-adjusted basis.
Client conversation: “Bexley earned more, but it took much bigger swings to do it. Per unit of risk, Alder did better, and it costs a fraction as much.”
🔬 Go deeper — why low R² breaks Treynor and Jensen+

Both use beta, which captures only market-related risk. When R² is low, most of the fund’s movement comes from other factors, so beta misstates its risk. Sharpe uses total risk (standard deviation) and works for any portfolio.

Decision 5 · Taxes: wash sales, TEY and placement (LO 1.4.1 · LO 7.4.1 · LO 3.6.1)

−

Three tax tools: avoid wash sales when harvesting losses, compare municipals on a taxable-equivalent basis, and place tax-inefficient holdings in tax-deferred accounts.

  • Wash sale — loss sale plus purchase of the same or similar securities within 30 days before or after (2026 edition figure): loss postponed and added to the new basis.
  • Net capital loss limit — $3,000 a year against other income, remainder carried forward (2026 edition figure).
  • Placement — taxable interest, nonqualified dividends, high turnover, taxable zeros → tax-deferred; index funds and munis → taxable.
TEY (federal only): tax-free yield ÷ (1 − federal rate)
TEY (federal + state exempt, itemizer): tax-free yield ÷ [(1 − state)(1 − federal)]
Non-itemizer: divide by 1 − (federal + state).
🧮 Worked solution — Q17

Sold Harborline for $85,000 (basis $100,000); bought it back 21 days later for $82,000.

  1. Realized loss: $85,000 − $100,000 = −$15,000.
  2. Repurchase within 30 days → recognized loss $0.
  3. New basis: $82,000 + $15,000 = $97,000. Answer (Q17): $0 loss; basis $97,000
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Treating the loss as gone for good; applying the $3,000 cap here.
🧮 Worked solution — Q18 and Q19

Muni 3.6% (double exempt), corporate 5.2%, 32% federal, 5% state, itemizers.

  1. Denominator: 0.95 × 0.68 = 0.646.
  2. TEY: 3.6% ÷ 0.646 = 5.57% > 5.2% → municipal. Answer (Q18): 5.57%; the municipal fund
  3. Same mix, less tax: bonds into the 401(k), stock index funds or munis in the joint account. Answer (Q19): Hold the taxable bond exposure in the 401(k) and keep tax-efficient index funds or municipals in the joint account
⚠️ Common wrong methods: 5.29% (federal only); 5.71% (brackets added for an itemizer); munis in a Roth.
⚖️ Commonly confused — TEY formulas
SituationDivide tax-free yield by
Federal-exempt only1 − F
Double-exempt, itemizer(1 − S)(1 − F)
Double-exempt, non-itemizer1 − (F + S)
How to tell: Check two facts first: is the bond in-state, and does the client itemize?
Exam trap: T-bill and Treasury interest is state-exempt; municipal interest is federal-exempt (and state-exempt only in-state). Use the right rate for each.
Client conversation: “For you, a 3.6% tax-free fund is worth about 5.6% taxable, so it beats the 5.2% bond fund. And bonds that pay taxable interest belong in your 401(k), not your brokerage account.”

Decision 6 · Bond risk and the college money (LO 4.1.2 · LO 2.3.1)

−

Duration measures a bond’s price sensitivity to rates and should line up with the goal’s time horizon. A bond fund never matures, so its price risk cannot be held away.

Price change: ΔP ÷ P = −D × [Δy ÷ (1 + y)]
where D = duration, Δy = change in rates, y = current YTM (not the coupon)
  • Immunization — portfolio duration = time horizon, so price risk and reinvestment risk offset.
  • Zero-coupon bond — duration equals maturity; accrued interest is taxed yearly in a taxable account (phantom income).
🧮 Worked solution — Q20

Ridgeway: $60,000, duration 6.2, YTM 5.2%, rates +1%.

  1. Δy ÷ (1 + y) = 0.01 ÷ 1.052 = 0.009506.
  2. −6.2 × 0.009506 = −5.89%.
  3. $60,000 × (1 − 0.0589) ≈ $56,464. Answer (Q20): $56,464
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Rule of thumb only ($56,280); wrong sign ($63,536).
Try it: What if rates fell 0.5%? −6.2 × (−0.005 ÷ 1.052) = +2.95% → about $61,768.
🧮 Worked solution — Q21

Leah’s $96,000 is in stocks; tuition due in 3 years.

  1. Money needed within 5 years should generally leave stocks.
  2. Match duration to 3 years: a 3-year Treasury zero has duration 3 and no default or reinvestment risk. Answer (Q21): Three-year Treasury zero-coupon bonds, whose duration equals the three-year horizon
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Reaching for yield with a long-duration fund; a barbell for a dated goal.
Exam trap: In the duration formula, y is the current yield to maturity, not the coupon rate.
Client conversation: “Leah’s money has a due date, so we buy something that comes due on that date. Then rate moves and market drops can’t change what she gets.”

Decision 7 · Currency and the European fund (LO 5.5.2)

−

A foreign holding’s dollar return combines its local return with the currency move. Think of the money traveling: it goes abroad at purchase and comes home at sale.

Dollar HPR: (1 + local return) × (ending $ per unit ÷ beginning $ per unit) − 1
🧮 Worked solution — Q22

$36,000 invested at €1 = $1.08; euro value +9%; now €1 = $1.02.

  1. $36,000 ÷ 1.08 = €33,333.
  2. €33,333 × 1.09 = €36,333.
  3. €36,333 × $1.02 = $37,060.
  4. ($37,060 − $36,000) ÷ $36,000 = 2.94%. Answer (Q22): 2.94%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Reporting 9% (local only); subtracting the currency loss (3.44%); inverting the rate (15.41%).
Try it: If the euro had risen to $1.12 instead: 1.09 × (1.12 ÷ 1.08) − 1 = 13.04%.
Exam trap: A U.S. investor in foreign securities wants the dollar to fall during the holding period; a stronger dollar reduces the return.
Client conversation: “Your European fund did well in euros, but the euro lost ground to the dollar, so you kept about 3% of that 9%. That currency swing is part of the diversification you’re buying.”
