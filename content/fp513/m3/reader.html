LO 3.1.1 · Common stock: claims, types and risks

−

Common stock is ownership. Owners share in assets and earnings and vote (usually by proxy), but get no promised payment. Bondholders are lenders: fixed payments, principal back, no ownership, and first claim in a default or liquidation.

  • Limited liability — the corporation is a separate legal entity, so shareholders aren't personally liable for its debts.
  • Stock-bond correlation — historically low and positive; the two compete for investor money.

🧮 Worked example — matching the stock to the worry

Rafael (61) fears a recession and wants one stock that holds up. Candidates: automaker, restaurant chain, robotics start-up, grocery chain.

  1. Automaker and restaurants are cyclical: earnings drop in a downturn.
  2. The robotics firm is emerging growth/technology: high risk.
  3. The grocery chain is defensive: demand for necessities holds up → best fit.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: picking a "safe-sounding" blue chip without checking whether its business is cyclical; confusing income stocks (dividend focus) with defensive stocks (cycle resistance).
Try it: A company funds a new plant with bonds instead of new shares. Which risk rises? Financial risk.
Exam trap: Stockholders do not rank ahead of bondholders in liquidation, and the issuer can't deduct dividends.
Client conversation: "When you own stock you own a slice of the business, so you share in the upside, but nobody owes you a payment. Some companies ride the economy up and down; others sell things people buy no matter what. We mix them so one bad stretch doesn't sink the plan."

LO 3.1.1 · Rights and warrants

−

Both give the right to buy the issuer's stock at a set price. Rights protect existing owners' percentage in a new offering. Warrants are long-dated sweeteners that make a bond or preferred issue easier to sell.

🧮 Worked example — exercising a warrant

Lena pays $2/share for warrants on 80 shares at a $25 exercise price. The stock reaches $31.

  1. Gain per share = $31 − $25 = $6.
  2. Net of warrant cost = $6 − $2 = $4.
  3. Profit = $4 × 80 = $320 (only $160 was at risk).
⚠️ Common wrong methods: forgetting to subtract the warrant cost ($480); adding it ($640).
Try it: Warrant cost $1.50, 120 shares, exercise $40, stock $46. Profit? ($6 − $1.50) × 120 = $540.
Exam trap: Warrants are customized, not standardized. And attaching them lets the issuer pay a lower coupon or dividend, cutting its cost of capital.
Client conversation: "These rights let you buy new shares at a discount so your ownership isn't watered down. Use them, sell them, but don't just let them lapse; they expire in a few weeks."

LO 3.2.1 · Cash dividends, dates, DRIPs and taxation

−

A dividend exists only once the board declares it, unlike bond interest, which is owed. Common holders are paid after preferred holders. Only shareholders of record on the record date receive it.

  • Ex-dividend date — first trading day a buyer does not get the declared dividend. Buy before it.
  • Record date — settled owners on the company's books are the holders of record. The book places it one business day after the ex-date under T+1 settlement (adopted May 2024) (2026 edition figure).
  • Payout ratio — dividends per share ÷ EPS.
  • DRIP — dividends buy more shares straight from the issuer, little or no commission, sometimes at a discount; extra cash often allowed.
Dividend payout ratio = dividend per share ÷ earnings per share
🧮 Worked example — payout and timing

Halvern earns $3.20 per share and pays out 40%. Its ex-dividend date is a Wednesday.

  1. Dividend = $3.20 × 40% = $1.28 per share.
  2. Mateo must buy by Tuesday. Any Wednesday purchase, even at the open, comes without the dividend.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing EPS by the payout ratio ($8.00); thinking a buy on the ex-date morning still qualifies.
Try it: EPS $4.50, payout 30%. Dividend? $1.35.

🔬 Go deeper — why the book's date rule needs care+

The book's dated example counts back one business day from the record date to find the ex-date, then says to buy the day before that. The rule that never changes is buy before the ex-dividend date. If an exam item gives you the ex-date, use it directly. If it gives only the record date, follow the book's one-day count-back.

−

All three change the share count and price per share but not the company's value or the holder's ownership percentage. The corporation's assets and liabilities don't change, so it is a recapitalization.

🧮 Worked example — 20% stock dividend and a 3-for-2 split

Tallis: worth $24M, 2M shares ($12). Pays a 20% stock dividend. Separately, Priya owns 200 Norland shares (cost $45, price $90) before a 3-for-2 split.

  1. Tallis shares: 2M × 1.20 = 2.4M → price $24M ÷ 2.4M = $10.
  2. Priya's shares: 200 × 3 ÷ 2 = 300; price $90 × 2 ÷ 3 = $60; value still $18,000.
  3. Basis: $9,000 ÷ 300 = $30 per share.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: cutting price by the dividend % ($12 × 0.8 = $9.60); treating 3-for-2 as 2-for-1; leaving basis per share unchanged.
Try it: 1,000 shares at $2 go through a 1-for-5 reverse split. 200 shares at $10.
Exam trap: A stock dividend becomes taxable if the holder could have chosen cash (constructive receipt).
Client conversation: "The split didn't make you richer. You have twice the shares at half the price: same pie, smaller slices. Your cost per share drops too, which matters when you sell."

LO 3.3.1 · Open-end funds: NAV, share classes and expenses

−

A mutual fund pools money for diversification and professional management under the objective in its prospectus. It sells unlimited new shares and redeems them itself; shares don't trade on exchanges.

NAV per share = (total assets − liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding
Total assets = cash + current value of securities (everything the fund owns)
  • Pricing — at least once per business day, usually after the 4:00 p.m. ET NYSE close.
  • Forward pricing — an order gets the next NAV computed after it is placed.
  • Prospectus — fees, expenses, objectives, strategies, risks, performance, pricing.
🧮 Worked example — NAV per share

Aldergrove Fund: securities $46.1M, cash $2.4M, liabilities $1.3M, 2.36M shares.

  1. Assets = $46.1M + $2.4M = $48.5M.
  2. Net = $48.5M − $1.3M = $47.2M.
  3. Per share = $47.2M ÷ 2.36M = $20.00.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: leaving out cash ($18.98); forgetting liabilities ($20.55).
Try it: Assets $5.2M, liabilities $0.2M, 400,000 shares. $12.50.

AFront-end load added at purchaseGenerally lowest total cost; suits long horizons
BBack-end load (CDSC) on early saleMay be waived after ~5 years
CLevel load: the annual 12b-1 fee is the sales chargeAsset-based; charged every year

  • 12b-1 fee — meant for marketing; now mostly ongoing pay for selling the fund.

🧮 Worked example — a UIT that ends early

A bond UIT starts with 15-year bonds. In year 5 rates fall and most issuers call their bonds, leaving 12% of the original principal. Its prospectus allows termination below 25%.

  1. Call proceeds are paid out to unit holders as they come in.
  2. Principal falls below the 25% trigger → the trust can liquidate.
  3. Holders get their principal back years earlier than planned.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: assuming a bond UIT always runs to its stated maturity; thinking UIT holders defer tax until termination.
Try it: Why can a closed-end manager hold illiquid bonds more easily than a mutual fund manager? No daily redemptions to fund.
Exam trap: Closed-end investors can profit four ways: distributed income, distributed gains, share price gains, and a narrowing discount (or widening premium).
Client conversation: "In a separately managed account you own each stock yourself, so we can keep out companies you don't want and choose which lots to sell for taxes. A mutual fund can't do that for you."

LO 3.4.1 · ETFs, hedge funds, funds of funds and GICs

−

ETFs are mostly index-based funds that trade all day like stock. Hedge funds are private, actively managed pools for wealthy, sophisticated investors. GICs are insurer-guaranteed, CD-like contracts used mostly in retirement plans.

  • ETF tracking — replicate (holds every index security) or sample (representative subset).
  • ETF varieties — index, sector, bond, commodity, active or thematic, and leveraged/inverse (2×, −1×, −2× daily).
  • In-kind redemption — investor receives underlying stock, not cash, so it isn't taxed.
  • Indexing — passive: match the index, less costs; low expenses and turnover.

CapitalizationUnlimitedFixedFixedVaries

PriceNAV + any loadMarket valueNAV + commissionMarket price near NAV + commission

Hedge fund strategies: arbitrage, distressed securities, long-short, global macro, venture/private/IPO deals, event-driven, short selling, leverage, derivatives.

Hedge fund risks: leverage, unlimited short-sale losses, high-risk holdings (e.g., subprime-based debt), little transparency.

  • Structure — usually a limited partnership: the manager is the GP, investors are LPs, mostly accredited.
  • Manager pay — performance-based (a mutual fund manager is paid on assets); often a big personal stake.
  • Fund of funds — about 10–30 hedge funds; diversification, often more liquidity, entry-level, opens access to nonaccredited investors. Costs: a second fee layer and cash drag. 🧮 Worked example — picking the vehicle

Sofia wants cheap S&P 500 exposure she can trade at noon, buy on margin and short. Pellham's 401(k) trustee wants a GIC and expects rates to climb.

  1. Intraday trading + margin + shorting → an index ETF, not an index mutual fund.
  2. Rising-rate outlook → participating GIC, whose rate follows market rates up.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: calling GICs risk-free (no FDIC coverage); assuming ETFs are always passive.
Try it: A widow with $300,000 who needs monthly income asks about a hedge fund. Fit? No: hedge funds are for wealthy, sophisticated investors.
Exam trap: A GIC is not FDIC-insured, so it is slightly riskier than a CD despite the word "guaranteed." Hedge funds are largely unregulated, but most investors must be accredited.
Client conversation: "The stable-value option in your 401(k) is backed by an insurance company, not the FDIC. It's conservative, but its safety depends on that insurer."

LO 3.5.1 · Averaging techniques

−

Three formula plans ease money into the market. They differ in what stays fixed each period: dollars (DCA), account-value growth (value averaging) or share count (share averaging).

PlanFixed each periodFalling priceCan sell?Suits
Dollar cost averagingDollars investedBuys more sharesNoSimple, automatic (e.g., 401(k)); value-style
Value averagingTarget rise in account valueInvests even moreYes, if above targetHands-on investors willing to buy heavily in declines
Share averagingShares boughtSpends lessNoCan raise contributions; momentum-style
Average cost per share = total dollars invested ÷ total shares bought
Average price per share = sum of purchase prices ÷ number of purchases
Value averaging investment = target value this period − (shares held × current price)
🧮 Worked example — DCA: average cost vs. average price

Jun invests $3,000 a month at $20, $15, $24, $16.

  1. Shares: 150 + 200 + 125 + 187.5 = 662.5.
  2. Average cost = $12,000 ÷ 662.5 = $18.11.
  3. Average price = $75 ÷ 4 = $18.75. Cost is lower because more shares were bought at $15 and $16.
  4. Value at $16 = $10,600 → a $1,400 loss. DCA lowers cost; it doesn't guarantee a profit.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: reporting the simple average price as average cost; assuming DCA locks in a gain.
Try it: $1,000 a month at $10, then $5. Average cost? $2,000 ÷ 300 = $6.67 (average price $7.50).

LO 3.5.1 · Cost basis methods for fund shares

−

Gain or loss = net sale proceeds − adjusted basis. When shares were bought at different times and prices, the basis method decides which cost is matched to the shares sold.

MethodHowRising market
Specific identificationName the exact lots sold; needs recordsMost favorable (pick high-cost lots)
Average costTotal cost ÷ total shares; every share has the same basisIn between; most commonly used
FIFOEarliest shares are sold first; IRS defaultLeast favorable (oldest = cheapest)

🧮 Worked example — one sale, three methods

Dmitri's lots: 200 @ $10, 300 @ $14, 250 @ $18 (750 shares, $10,700). He sells 200 at $20 ($4,000).

  1. FIFO: basis 200 × $10 = $2,000 → gain $2,000.
  2. Average cost: $10,700 ÷ 750 = $14.2667; basis $2,853.33 → gain $1,146.67.
  3. Specific ID ($18 lot): basis $3,600 → gain $400.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: averaging the three lot prices ($14.00) instead of weighting by shares; leaving reinvested dividends out of basis.
Try it: Rhea paid $9,750 + $250 commission for 500 shares and reinvested $600 of dividends (30 shares). She sells all 530 at $23. Gain? $12,190 − $10,600 = $1,590.
Exam trap: Reinvested exempt-interest dividends from a muni fund also raise basis, even though they were never taxed. Selling muni fund shares is still a taxable event.
Client conversation: "Every reinvested dividend is money you already paid tax on, so it counts as part of what you paid. If we pick which shares to sell instead of letting the oldest go first, we can shrink the taxable gain."

LO 3.6.1 · Fund selection: objective, holdings, flexibility and risk

−

Start with the client, not the fund. Rank the client's objectives, match the fund's objective and risk to them, then confirm what the fund actually owns and how much freedom its manager has.

  • Objectives — income, appreciation, preservation; rank them, since one fund can't do all three at once.
  • Portfolio view — total portfolio risk matters more than one fund's risk; a volatile fund with low correlation can fit a cautious client.
  • Strategy disclosure — strategies that drive returns must be in the prospectus; over 5% of assets is generally material to the SEC. Watch leverage, short selling, securities lending, concentration, manager flexibility, style.
  • Holdings — check annual and semiannual reports plus quarterly holdings. ≥40% in one sector (e.g., tech) = aggressive; no industry above 15% = more conservative; a heavy top-10 = more risk.
  • Permitted securities — derivatives, illiquid/restricted, foreign, high-yield, small-cap.
  • Risk — far outperforming peers can signal above-average risk; read the prospectus risk section.
🧮 Worked example — manager flexibility changes the real allocation

Ava puts 30% of her portfolio in what she believes is an all-stock fund. The manager is actually 75% stocks / 25% cash.

  1. Stock through this fund = 30% × 75% = 22.5%.
  2. Cash through this fund = 30% × 25% = 7.5%.
  3. Her plan is more conservative than intended. Pick funds with clear limits, or adjust.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: trusting the fund's label; judging aggressiveness from the prospectus (what it may own) rather than actual holdings.
Try it: 40% in a "U.S. growth" fund that is 80% U.S. / 20% international. U.S. stock exposure? 32%.

LO 3.6.1 · Performance, fees, turnover, taxes and pitfalls

−

Past returns don't reliably predict future returns, but consistency, costs and tax drag are measurable and persistent. Judge funds over full cycles, against true peers, and after risk.

Break-even gain after a loss = loss ÷ (1 − loss)
Expense ratio = (management + 12b-1 + other operating expenses) ÷ average assets
Portfolio turnover = lesser of purchases or sales ÷ average monthly assets
🧮 Worked example — consistency and costs

$10,000 each in Fund X (+20%, −10%) and Fund Y (+40%, −25%). Fund X: fees 0.55% + 0.25% + 0.15%, 4% front load; bought $120M, sold $90M, average assets $300M.

  1. X = $10,000 × 1.20 × 0.90 = $10,800; Y = $10,000 × 1.40 × 0.75 = $10,500.
  2. A 40% drop needs 0.40 ÷ 0.60 = 66.67% to recover.
  3. Expense ratio = 0.95% (the load is excluded).
  4. Turnover = $90M ÷ $300M = 30%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: adding percentages instead of compounding; putting loads or trading commissions in the expense ratio; using the larger of purchases or sales.
Try it: A fund drops 25%. Gain to break even? 33.33%.

⚖️ Commonly confused — turnover vs. tax efficiency
BeliefReality
High turnover always means big gainsRealized losses can offset gains
Low turnover always means tax-efficientLarge embedded gains can still be realized
Funds pass losses throughLosses stay in the fund to offset future gains
How to tell: look at embedded gains and loss carryforwards, not just turnover.
  • Survivorship bias — failed funds merged or closed away make category averages look too good.
  • Buying the distribution — buying just before a big year-end capital gain distribution buys a tax bill.
  • Hidden loads — 12b-1 fees inside "no-load" funds; a CDSC mistaken for no-load.
  • New funds with great records — hot IPO allocations can dominate a small fund's returns.
  • "#1 ranked" — check the category, period and number of funds.
  • Misleading 10-year average — best years may be early, before most money arrived; read year by year.
  • Investor vs. fund return — chasing past winners means investors earn less than the fund. Reported returns are pretax.
  • Redemption spiral — a bad year triggers selling and gains spread over fewer remaining holders.
Exam trap: Growth funds can produce large taxable distributions in bull years even if you never sell. Short-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates. Tax-inefficient funds belong in tax-deferred accounts.
Client conversation: "This fund looks great on a one-year chart, but it lost far more than its peers in the last downturn, and its fees and trading costs eat about 1% a year before taxes. The steadier, cheaper fund has actually left investors with more money."
