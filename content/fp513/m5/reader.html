LO 5.1.1 · Direct real estate: NOI, cap rates, leverage, raw land

−

Real assets (real estate, gold, collectibles) can be touched and often have low or negative correlation with stocks and bonds. A home is a personal-use asset on the balance sheet; investment real estate is bought for income or appreciation. Its two big draws: leverage (mostly borrowed money) and tax deductions (mortgage interest, property taxes, depreciation).

  • PGI — potential gross income: all rents plus other income (laundry, parking)
  • EGI — effective gross income: PGI minus vacancy and collection losses
  • NOI — EGI minus cash operating expenses; never subtract depreciation or debt service
  • Cap rate — the discount rate used to turn NOI into value; estimated from comparable recent sales, then adjusted
  • Undeveloped land — passive, no income, ongoing costs; held only for large appreciation
NOI ladder: Rents + other income = PGI → − vacancy/collection = EGI → − cash operating expenses = NOI
Value: V = NOI ÷ cap rate
Leveraged return on equity: (sale price − purchase price) ÷ down payment
🧮 Worked example — Maple Court (fictional)

20 units at $1,200/month; other income $6,000/yr; vacancy 6% of PGI; cash operating expenses $98,000; mortgage interest $61,000; depreciation $25,000. Comparable cap rate 7%.

  1. Gross rents = 20 × $1,200 × 12 = $288,000; PGI = $288,000 + $6,000 = $294,000
  2. Vacancy = 6% × $294,000 = $17,640 → EGI = $276,360
  3. NOI = $276,360 − $98,000 = $178,360 (interest and depreciation ignored)
  4. Value = $178,360 ÷ 0.07 = $2,548,000 — the most a prudent buyer should pay
⚠️ Common wrong methods: subtracting mortgage interest or depreciation; skipping vacancy; multiplying NOI by the cap rate; capitalizing EGI instead of NOI.
Try it: If comparable sales point to an 8% cap rate instead, what is Maple Court worth? ($178,360 ÷ 0.08 = $2,229,500 — a higher cap rate means a lower value.)

How to tell: if the property earns nothing and the only payoff is appreciation, it is raw land — extremely risky, so it needs a large expected gain.
Exam trap: NOI is about the property's cash flow, not the investor's. Any answer choice that subtracts the mortgage payment or depreciation is wrong.
Client conversation: “Before you fall in love with the building, let's see what it actually earns after vacancies and running costs. Divide that by what similar buildings sold for relative to their income, and you have the ceiling on what to pay.”
🔬 Go deeper — why emotions matter more in residential deals+

Commercial property (offices, retail, restaurants) is the domain of experienced investors who hire appraisers; residential property (houses, apartments, condos, hotels) draws less experienced buyers who sometimes buy for personal satisfaction. The planner's job is to keep the decision on NOI and value. Leverage cuts both ways: the same 10× magnifier turns a 10% price drop into a 100% loss of equity.

LO 5.1.1 · Indirect real estate: RELPs, REITs, REMICs and CMOs

−

RELPs and REITs were created so investors could own real estate without the high cost and illiquidity of buying buildings directly. REMICs and CMOs are mortgage-based vehicles.

  • REIT dividends — generally ordinary income; the text notes they may qualify for the 0%/15%/20% rates (2026 edition figure)

🧮 Worked example — matching a CMO tranche

A CMO has Tranches A, B, C and Z. Every tranche except Z gets interest now; principal goes only to A until A is paid off, then to B, then C. Z receives nothing until the others are retired.

  1. A client with a short horizon who wants the least rate risk → Tranche A (shortest repayment)
  2. Tranche Z behaves like a zero-coupon bond: interest accrues and is taxed as earned, even with no cash received
  3. Longest wait = most interest rate risk → Tranche Z
⚠️ Common wrong methods: assuming each tranche gets a pro rata share of principal (that is a plain MBS pass-through); thinking Z is safest because it is “last.”
Try it: Which tranche would create taxable income without paying cash? (Tranche Z.)
Exam trap: REIT shareholders are not subject to the passive activity rules — that limit belongs to RELP investors. And the REIT is run by a board, not a general partner.
Client conversation: “A REIT lets you own a slice of many buildings for the price of a few shares, and you can sell tomorrow. A limited partnership can lock your money up for years, so it only fits money you truly won't need.”
🔬 Go deeper — how to evaluate a REIT+

Reasons to own REITs: high income, diversification, low correlation with stocks and bonds, inflation protection (equity REITs) and global reach (more than 30 countries have REIT laws). Check how diversified a REIT is by property type and region, management's philosophy (property focus, leverage, income vs. growth), and the usual stock/bond factors: cash flow, track record across cycles, manager tenure, buy/sell discipline and expense ratio.

LO 5.2.1 · Option basics, payoffs, intrinsic and time value

−

A derivative takes its value from another asset. An option gives the buyer (holder) a right — not an obligation — to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at the strike (exercise) price on or before expiration. The writer (seller) collects the premium and takes on the obligation. One contract = 100 shares. The Options Clearing Corporation guarantees listed contracts. Most options run under a year; LEAPS run for years.

PositionViewMax gainMax lossBreakeven
Buy callBullishUnlimitedPremiumStrike + premium
Write call (naked)Bearish/neutralPremiumUnlimitedStrike + premium
Buy putBearishStrike − premiumPremiumStrike − premium
Write putBullish/neutralPremiumStrike − premiumStrike − premium

Long and short positions on the same option are mirror images: options are a zero-sum game, so the buyer's gain is the writer's loss.

Call intrinsic value = greater of 0 or (stock price − strike)
Put intrinsic value = greater of 0 or (strike − stock price)
Time value = premium − intrinsic value (premium = intrinsic + time)
  • In the money — intrinsic value > 0 (call: stock above strike; put: stock below strike)
  • At the money — stock price equals strike; no intrinsic value
  • Out of the money — zero intrinsic value and stock ≠ strike (call: stock below; put: stock above)
🧮 Worked example — Kestrel stock at $52 (fictional)
  1. $50 call priced $4.10: intrinsic = $52 − $50 = $2.00; time value = $2.10; in the money; buyer's breakeven $54.10
  2. $55 put priced $4.60: intrinsic = $55 − $52 = $3.00; time value = $1.60; in the money; buyer's breakeven $50.40
  3. A $55 call would be out of the money (intrinsic $0); a $50 put also out of the money
  4. Writer of the $55 put: max gain $460 per contract; max loss ($55 − $4.60) × 100 = $5,040
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using stock − strike for a put; letting intrinsic value go negative; adding (not subtracting) the premium in a put breakeven; forgetting ×100 per contract.
Try it: Kestrel falls to $47. What is the $50 put's intrinsic value? ($3.00.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — in the money vs. profitable
In the moneyProfitable for the buyer
CallStock > strikeStock > strike + premium
PutStock < strikeStock < strike − premium
How to tell: moneyness ignores the premium; profit does not.
Exam trap: the long put's gain is not unlimited — it is capped at strike minus premium because a stock cannot go below zero. Only the long call (gain) and naked short call (loss) are unlimited.
Client conversation: “Buying an option is like paying for a reservation: you can walk away and lose only the deposit. Selling one is like taking the deposit — you keep it, but you must honor the deal if the buyer shows up.”
🔬 Go deeper — reading the hockey-stick graphs+

At expiration, a long call is flat at −premium until the stock passes the strike, then slopes up to the right. A long put is flat at −premium above the strike and slopes up to the left as the stock falls. The short versions are the same lines flipped upside down: flat at +premium, sloping down past the strike.

LO 5.2.1 · Option pricing models and option taxation

−

You will not calculate an option price on the exam; know the models' assumptions, the direction each input pushes a call's value, and how premiums hit basis or gain.

Binomial (two-state): the stock moves up or down in discrete steps over periods; more steps → the result approaches Black-Scholes.

Black-Scholes: values a European call assuming prices change continuously (geometric Brownian motion), captured by volatility (σ).

🧮 Worked example — basis after exercise
  1. Lia wrote a $45 put for $2. It is exercised: she buys 100 shares at $45. Basis = ($45 − $2) × 100 = $4,300
  2. Her neighbor bought a $45 call for $2 and exercised: basis = ($45 + $2) × 100 = $4,700
  3. Had the put expired instead, Lia reports a $200 short-term capital gain

Try it: A call writer's shares are called away at $60; premium was $3. Amount realized per share? ($63.)
Exam trap: a higher strike lowers a call's value because the stock must climb further to have intrinsic value. Everything else in the Black-Scholes list moves with the call's value.
Client conversation: “If your call is worth more than its exercise value, sell it rather than exercising early — exercising throws away the time value someone else will pay you for.”
🔬 Go deeper — why models use European options+

Early exercise of an American option sacrifices its time value, so a rational holder sells it instead. Because early exercise is rarely optimal, pricing models are built on European options, with variations that also apply to American ones.

LO 5.2.2 · Using options: speculation, covered calls, protective puts

−

The three most common uses: buying options to speculate, writing covered calls for income, and buying protective puts to hedge.

  • Speculation — more leverage than margin (margin needs at least 50% cash under Fed rules, 2026 edition figure; an option may cost ~5% of the stock); loss capped at premium; but options expire, so the move must come in time
  • Covered call — own the shares, write calls; keep the premium, give up gains above the strike; best in a sideways market
  • Premium drivers — stock price vs. strike, time left, volatility
  • Protective put — own the shares, buy puts; floor at the strike; costly and temporary
  • Index puts — hedge a whole portfolio (“portfolio insurance”); match the index to the holdings
  • Buying time — buy a call (or put) to lock in a position while finishing research
  • Hedging a short sale — buy a call to cap the unlimited loss from a rising stock
🧮 Worked example — covered call vs. protective put

Nadia owns 200 shares bought at $30 and writes 2 calls, $33 strike, $1.20 premium ($240 total).

  1. Stock ends at $36: shares called at $33 → gain ($33 − $30 + $1.20) × 200 = $840; the move above $33 belongs to the buyer
  2. Stock ends at $31: calls expire; she keeps the $240 and the shares
  3. Stock ends at $25: loss ($25 − $30 + $1.20) × 200 = −$760; the premium cushions only $1.20/share. Breakeven = $30 − $1.20 = $28.80
  4. Protective put instead: own at $50, buy a $47 put for $1.50 → max loss = ($50 − $47) + $1.50 = $4.50/share; upside kept, less $1.50
⚠️ Common wrong methods: letting the covered call writer keep gains above the strike; forgetting the put premium when computing the floor; calling covered calls “safe” in a crash.
Try it: A speculator buys a $40 call for $1.50. Above what price does she profit at expiration? ($41.50.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — covered call vs. protective put
Covered callProtective put
Option actionWrite (sell) a callBuy a put
CashReceives premiumPays premium
UpsideCapped at strikeKept (less premium)
DownsideCushioned only by premiumFloored at strike
How to tell: goal is income → write calls; goal is protection → buy puts.
Exam trap: a short seller hedges with a call, not a put — the risk is a rising price.
Client conversation: “Selling calls on a stock you already own pays you rent while you wait. The catch: if it takes off, you sell at the agreed price and miss the rest — so only do it on shares you'd be happy to part with.”
🔬 Go deeper — buying time with a call+

An investor who likes a $30 stock but needs weeks to finish research can buy a $31 call for $0.80. If the stock runs to $36, exercising gets shares at $31 (all-in $31.80) instead of chasing at $36. If it drops to $28, the call lapses and the all-in cost of buying then is $28.80 — still below buying at $30 today.

🧮 Worked example — straddle and collar
  1. Long straddle at $40: call $2.20 + put $1.80 = $4.00 → profits only below $36 or above $44
  2. Zero-cost collar: Yusuf's stock (bought at $40) is now $90. He buys an $85 put for $3 and writes a $100 call for $3 → net cost $0; value locked between $85 and $100 until expiration

Client conversation: “You have a big gain in one stock. A collar puts a floor under it and pays for that floor by agreeing to sell if it rises past a ceiling — protection without writing a check.”
🔬 Go deeper — cutting a losing naked call+

A naked call writer facing a rising stock can buy back the same call to close the position and stop further losses. The writer keeps the premium no matter what; the danger is the open obligation until the call is bought back or expires.

LO 5.3.1 · Futures: terms, margin, hedging and taxation

−

A futures contract obligates two parties to make or take delivery of a set amount of a commodity or financial asset (Treasuries, indices, currencies) at a future date and price. Contracts are standardized and exchange-traded (e.g., CME, NYMEX); only about 1% end in delivery — most are closed with an offsetting contract. Long = agree to buy (gains if price rises); short = agree to sell (gains if price falls). Unlike an option buyer, a futures trader can lose far more than the deposit.

  • Initial margin — good faith deposit / performance bond, roughly 5–10% of contract value; hedgers post less than speculators because they can deliver
  • Maintenance margin — if equity drops below it, deposit enough to restore the initial margin, immediately
  • Marked to market — daily gains/losses posted to account equity
  • Spot price — cash price now; futures converge to it at expiration
  • Daily price limit — max move from prior settlement
  • Open interest — contracts outstanding
  • Short hedge — own the asset, sell futures (farmer, rancher, stock or bond holder)
  • Long hedge — need the asset later, buy futures (baker, cereal maker, meat packer)
Gain/loss = (new price − old price) × contract size × number of contracts (sign flips for a short)
Index futures = index-point change × multiplier × contracts
Tax: year-end net gain/loss = 60% long-term + 40% short-term capital (2026 edition figure)
🧮 Worked example — a margin call (fictional contract)

A speculator goes long one 1,000-barrel contract at $70. Initial margin $6,000; maintenance $4,500.

  1. Price settles at $68.20: loss = $1.80 × 1,000 = $1,800
  2. Equity = $6,000 − $1,800 = $4,200 < $4,500 → margin call
  3. Deposit to restore initial margin: $6,000 − $4,200 = $1,800 (not just $300)
  4. Index example: long 1 contract (multiplier 250) from 4,500 to 4,520 → +20 × $250 = $5,000
  5. If year-end net futures gain is $9,000: $5,400 long-term, $3,600 short-term
⚠️ Common wrong methods: topping up only to maintenance; forgetting the contract count or multiplier; using actual holding period for tax.
Try it: A pension manager fears rising interest rates on a bond portfolio. Buy or sell bond futures? (Sell.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — short hedge vs. long hedge
Short hedgeLong hedge
Position in the assetLong (owns it)Short (needs it)
Futures actionSellBuy
Protects againstFalling pricesRising prices
How to tell: the hedge is always opposite to the cash position. If the business would simply wait for lower prices, no long hedge is needed.
Exam trap: futures gains are capital gains even when the underlying (corn, cattle) would be inventory, and the 60/40 split applies regardless of how long the contract was held.
Client conversation: “Futures are like a deposit on a deal that settles every single day. Small moves can wipe out the deposit and demand more money overnight, which is why most advisors use them only for hedging or through managed accounts.”
🔬 Go deeper — why hedgers lock in, not profit+

Futures markets move price risk from producers and processors to speculators. A hedger's gain or loss on the futures offsets the opposite result in the cash market, so the hedger neither profits nor loses from later price moves — the price is locked. Index futures (S&P 500, Russell 2000, Nasdaq 100, MidCap 400) and Treasury note/bond futures let advisors hedge stock and bond portfolios; multipliers vary by contract.

LO 5.4.1 · Tangible assets: collectibles, gold, natural resources

−

Tangibles are collectibles (cards, coins, stamps, art) and precious metals. Collectibles offer enjoyment plus possible appreciation but trade in inefficient, illiquid markets. Precious metals get value from forces outside financial markets (e.g., fear of inflation), so they often move opposite to stocks — a hedge for a bearish stock market.

  • Collectibles tax rate — max 28% long-term (2026 edition figure), higher than for securities
  • Tax shelter traits — no tax until sale; usually no current income; step-up in basis at death
  • Gold's enemy — high real interest rates (no income, high opportunity cost); negative real rates help
  • Direct gold — bullion or bullion coins near spot price; storage burden; return only from appreciation; check how gold ETFs are built (actual metal vs. futures)
  • Gold miners — ~2× as volatile as large caps; foreign, currency and cost risks plus fly-by-night operators — prefer funds or large established firms
  • Allocation — an advisor may cap gold at about 5–10% of a diversified portfolio
  • Natural resources — oil, gas, timber via LPs, company stocks, or sector funds; depletion and intangible drilling cost deductions pass through; high risk
🧮 Worked example — the 28% difference

Paolo realizes a $30,000 long-term gain. His long-term rate on securities is 15%.

  1. Gain from selling stock: $30,000 × 15% = $4,500
  2. Same gain from a coin collection, if taxed at the 28% cap: $30,000 × 28% = $8,400
  3. If he instead holds the coins until death, heirs get a stepped-up basis and the gain escapes capital gains tax
⚠️ Common wrong methods: applying the 0/15/20% securities rates to collectibles; assuming tangibles throw off taxable income each year.
Try it: Real rates are high and rising. Is that good or bad for gold? (Bad.)
Exam trap: a retiree heavy in tangibles faces liquidity risk — being forced to sell into a weak, illiquid market to raise income.
Client conversation: “Collect what you know and love, but keep enough in liquid investments that you never have to sell the collection in a down market to pay bills.”
🔬 Go deeper — why natural resources diversify+

Supply of a resource is fixed in the short run and slow and costly to expand, so prices jump when demand outruns supply — for example during inflationary, high-rate periods that hurt stocks and bonds, or when emerging economies grow fast. That has historically given natural resources some negative correlation with financial assets.

LO 5.5.1 · Foreign securities: risks, markets, funds and ADRs

−

Foreign investing adds diversification and return potential, but brings two extra risks: exchange rate (currency) risk and country risk (political events plus structural risks such as confiscatory treatment of profits or dividends). Foreign markets are often more volatile, yet their low correlation with U.S. stocks, bonds and each other lowers portfolio risk. Accounting standards differ, so ratios may not compare. Foreign tax withheld can be taken as a foreign tax credit or an itemized deduction.

Emerging marketsDeveloped markets
Low GDP and equity capitalization; questionable liquidityLarge capitalization; highly liquid markets
Currency-conversion and ownership restrictionsFew or no currency restrictions
High volatility, unstable institutions, high taxes/commissionsStable political and social institutions
Weak regulation, little transparency — but fast growthClear regulation, U.S.-like transparency
  • Frontier markets — the smaller emerging economies; even more risk and volatility
  • Ways to invest — foreign mutual funds/ETFs, closed-end funds, direct purchase abroad, ADRs
  • Global fund — U.S. + foreign (can overlap domestic holdings); international fund — non-U.S. only
  • Single-country funds — professional research, high volatility, major currency risk, goal is capital appreciation; regional funds diversify more but countries in a region correlate
  • ADR — U.S. bank trust receipt for foreign shares; trades in the U.S. in dollars; dividends converted from local currency, so currency risk remains; dividends can qualify for 0%/15%/20% rates if readily tradable on an established U.S. market (2026 edition figure)
🧮 Worked example — choosing the vehicle

Deshawn already owns three U.S. stock funds and wants foreign exposure without researching foreign companies himself.

  1. Global fund? Would repeat U.S. names he already owns → no
  2. Direct purchase abroad? Foreign currency, possibly illiquid exchanges, weaker reporting, local rules → too complex
  3. Diversified international fund (developed) plus a diversified emerging markets fund → best fit; low correlation among countries smooths results
⚠️ Common wrong methods: assuming ADRs remove currency risk; picking a global fund to diversify away from the U.S.; using one single-country fund as the whole foreign allocation.
Try it: Which is a developed-market trait: currency limits or many large-cap securities? (Many large-cap securities.)
Exam trap: ADRs trade and pay in dollars, but currency risk is not eliminated.
Client conversation: “Owning companies overseas means you also own their currency. Some years that helps, some years it hurts — but because those markets don't move in lockstep with ours, they tend to steady the whole portfolio.”
🔬 Go deeper — why diversified EM beats picking one country+

Emerging countries often have only a few companies liquid enough for funds, with one firm dominating each industry. Correlations among the countries are low, so one may be up while another is down; a diversified emerging markets fund captures that. Political risk — regime change, rigged elections, nationalization — is the key emerging market risk.

LO 5.5.2 · Exchange rates and dollar returns on foreign securities

−

Think of your money traveling. Before buying a foreign security, a strong dollar helps (it buys more foreign currency). While you hold it, you want the dollar to fall against the foreign currency, so the proceeds convert back into more dollars. The rule works both ways: any investor holding another country's securities wants the home currency to weaken during the holding period.

Reciprocal quote: foreign units per $1 = 1 ÷ (dollars per foreign unit)
Dollar cost = foreign price ÷ (foreign units per $1 at purchase)
Dollar proceeds = foreign sale value ÷ (foreign units per $1 at sale)
HPR in dollars = (proceeds − cost) ÷ cost
🧮 Worked example — U.K. shares in dollars

Buy shares for £8,000 when $1 = £0.80; sell for £8,400 when $1 = £0.75.

  1. Cost = £8,000 ÷ 0.80 = $10,000
  2. Proceeds = £8,400 ÷ 0.75 = $11,200
  3. HPR = ($11,200 − $10,000) ÷ $10,000 = 12% (vs. 5% in pounds) — the dollar weakened, adding return
⚠️ Common wrong methods: reporting the local-currency return; multiplying where you should divide; assuming a stronger dollar helps an investor who already owns foreign shares.
Try it: Same trade, but at sale $1 = £0.84. Dollar HPR? (£8,400 ÷ 0.84 = $10,000 → 0%.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — revaluation vs. devaluation of the dollar
Dollar revalued (stronger)Dollar devalued (weaker)
Foreign units per $1MoreFewer
Dollars per foreign unitFewerMore
Effect on U.S. owner of foreign stockHurtsHelps
How to tell: first restate the quote as “how much foreign currency does $1 buy?” then decide.
Exam trap: a quote of $1.25 per pound means $1 buys 0.80 pounds — flip it with 1 ÷ x before converting.
Client conversation: “Your European fund's stocks rose 6%, but you made less because the dollar strengthened. When the dollar weakens, the same holdings give you a boost — that's currency risk, and it cuts both ways.”
🔬 Go deeper — calculator flip+

On an HP 10bII+, enter the rate and press the 1/x function to flip between “dollars per foreign unit” and “foreign units per dollar”; pressing it again flips back. The text recommends always starting from how much foreign currency one dollar buys.
