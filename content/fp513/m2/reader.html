LO 2.1.1 · Bond vocabulary, maturity and ratings

−

A bond is a loan to a government, municipality or company: the issuer pays coupons and returns principal at maturity. The contract behind it is the indenture.

  • Indenture / covenants — the issuer–bondholder contract; covenants are its promises and prohibitions. A sinking fund sets money aside yearly to retire the debt, lowering default risk.
  • Registered / bearer / book-entry — pay the owner of record / pay whoever holds it / ownership kept electronically at a central depository.
  • Par (face) value — principal repaid at maturity; exam default $1,000
  • Coupon rate (nominal yield) — annual interest as a % of par; exam default: paid semiannually
  • Basis point — 1/100 of 1% (a 0.75% move = 75 bp).
  • Discount / premium — trading below / above par. Issued below par = original issue discount (OID).
  • Call provision — lets the issuer retire the bond early, usually above par, typically after rates fall.
  • Secured bond vs debenture — specific assets pledged vs unsecured (higher yield for the same issuer and term).
  • Term vs serial bond — one maturity date vs a series of maturities (municipalities are the biggest serial issuers).
Maturity bucketTypical range
Money market / cash equivalentUnder 1 year (e.g., T-bills, sold at a discount)
Short-term1–5 years
Intermediate-term5–10 years
Long-termOver 10 years

Longer term → more price volatility (all else equal). An outstanding bond drifts from long to short term over time, so its volatility falls.

GradeS&PMoody's
Investment — high gradeAAA–AAAaa–Aa
Investment — medium gradeA–BBB−A–Baa
Speculative (high-yield)BB+–BBa–B
Default zoneCCC–DCaa–C
  • Higher ratings come with profitability, little other debt and strong cash-flow coverage; heavy leverage and unstable earnings drag ratings down.
  • Upgrade → yield falls (price rises). Downgrade → yield rises (price falls). Markets often move before the agencies do.
  • High-yield bonds track the issuer's business prospects, so they correlate more with stocks — poor diversifiers for an all-stock portfolio.
🧮 Worked example — coupon and basis points

A $1,000 par bond has a 3.5% coupon. Its yield moves from 4.20% to 4.95%.

  1. Annual coupon = 3.5% × $1,000 = $35; semiannual payment = $17.50.
  2. Yield change = 0.75 percentage points = 75 basis points.
  3. Yield above coupon → the bond trades at a discount.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: paying the full $35 every six months; calling 0.75% “7.5 bp.”
Try it: A yield falls from 5.10% to 4.60%. How many basis points? (50 bp)
Exam trap: the investment-grade line is BBB− / Baa. BB+ sounds close but is junk.
Client conversation: “The rating is a grade on how likely the issuer is to pay you back. Below BBB you're paid more because the odds of a missed payment are higher — and those bonds tend to fall with stocks, so they won't cushion your equity risk.”

−

Bond value = PV of coupons + PV of principal. Anything that changes the discount rate or threatens the cash flows is a risk.

🧮 Worked example — currency risk

A U.S. investor buys a €10,000 bond when €1 = $1.10 (cost $11,000). At maturity €1 = $1.04.

  1. Proceeds = €10,000 × $1.04 = $10,400.
  2. Currency loss = $11,000 − $10,400 = $600 — the bond paid in full, the dollar strengthened.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: thinking a stronger dollar helps; it shrinks foreign-currency proceeds.
Try it: Same bond, but €1 = $1.20 at maturity. (+$1,000 gain)
Exam trap: Treasuries are default-risk free, not risk free — they still carry interest rate, reinvestment rate and purchasing power risk.
Client conversation: “A bond can lose value even if the issuer never misses a payment. If rates go up, newer bonds pay more, so yours is worth less if you sell before maturity.”

LO 2.1.1 · Treasuries, STRIPS, TIPS and savings bonds

−

Federal debt: default-risk free; interest taxed federally as ordinary income but exempt from state and local income tax.

SecurityKey featuresTax twist
T-notes / T-bondsNotes 2, 3, 5, 7, 10 yrs; bonds 30 yrs; issued at par via auction; semiannual coupons; $100 minimum and increments (2026 edition figure); quoted as % of par, bid-askOID issued after 7/1/1982 accrues yearly (phantom income) and raises basis
STRIPSEach coupon and the principal of a T-note/bond sold as separate zeros; via brokers/dealers, not the Treasury; deep discount; very rate-sensitive; no call riskAccrued interest taxed yearly → best in tax-deferred accounts

Selling a Treasury before maturity splits proceeds into: basis recovered tax free (cost + accrued OID), accrued interest from the buyer (interest income), and the rest as capital gain or loss.

🧮 Worked example — STRIPS and TIPS

(a) A $20,000, 5-year, 3% T-note is stripped. (b) Someone buys $20,000 of a 1.8% TIPS; CPI rises 2% in the first six months, then 1% in the next six.

  1. (a) 10 semiannual coupons of $20,000 × 3% ÷ 2 = $300, plus one $20,000 principal zero → 11 separate zeros.
  2. (b) Principal after six months = $20,000 × 1.02 = $20,400; payment = $20,400 × 0.9% = $183.60.
  3. Next six months: $20,400 × 1.01 = $20,604; payment = $20,604 × 0.9% = $185.44.
  4. Each $400 and $204 principal step-up is federal ordinary income in the year it happens.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: applying the full 1.8% to a half-year; paying the coupon on original par; forgetting the step-up raises basis.
Try it: $10,000 of a 2.4% TIPS, CPI +1% in six months — first payment? ($10,100 × 1.2% = $121.20)
Exam trap: TIPS keep a fixed rate; the dollar payment changes because the principal changes. And STRIPS can't be bought directly from the Treasury.
Client conversation: “TIPS grow your principal with inflation, so each payment grows too. The catch: the IRS taxes that growth every year even though you don't see it until maturity — so we'd rather hold them in your IRA.”
🔬 Go deeper — TIPS basis after a step-up+

Because the inflation step-up was already taxed, it is added to basis. Example: bought at $1,000, principal adjusted to $1,025, sold for $1,031 → $25 ordinary income in the adjustment year and only a $6 capital gain on sale.

LO 2.1.1 · Municipal bonds and taxable equivalent yield

−

Muni interest is generally exempt from regular federal income tax; it is usually state-tax free only for residents of the issuing state (if the indenture allows).

TypeRepaid fromRisk / yield
General obligation (GO)Taxing power (full faith and credit); usually voter-approved; debt limitsLowest risk
Revenue bondIncome of the financed facility (e.g., airport)Riskier → higher yield
Private activity bond>10% of proceeds for private business use and >10% of debt service tied to that property (2026 edition figure)Most credit risk; nonexempt ones are federally taxable
Insured muniInsurance against defaultLower credit risk; lets issuer cut the coupon

Revenue bond flavors: industrial development, special assessment, special tax, and new housing authority (Section 8). Qualified private activity bonds stay tax-exempt if 95%+ of net proceeds go to a qualified purpose (2026 edition figure).

Taxable equivalent yield: TEY = tax-exempt yield ÷ (1 − marginal tax rate)
After-tax yield: taxable yield × (1 − marginal tax rate)
🧮 Worked example — muni vs corporate

Client's marginal rate 24%. Muni yields 3.3%; a corporate yields 5.0%.

  1. TEY = 3.3% ÷ (1 − 0.24) = 3.3% ÷ 0.76 = 4.34%.
  2. Corporate after-tax = 5.0% × 0.76 = 3.80% (vs 3.3% muni).
  3. Either way the corporate wins at 24%: 5.0% > 4.34%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying the muni yield by (1 − t); adding state tax to an in-state muni; forgetting an out-of-state muni is state-taxed.
Try it: Same muni at a 35% rate. (3.3% ÷ 0.65 = 5.08% → muni now wins)
Exam trap: with state tax in the problem, tax each bond only where it's taxable: Treasuries (federal only), in-state munis (neither), out-of-state munis (state only), corporates (both).
Client conversation: “A 3.3% tax-free bond is like earning about 4.3% taxable in your bracket. The higher your bracket, the better munis look — the textbook's rule of thumb is to run the numbers at 24% and up (2026 edition figure).”

🧮 Worked example — market discount at sale

A par-issued bond is bought for $940. At sale for $990, accrued market discount is $35 (no annual election).

  1. Total gain = $990 − $940 = $50.
  2. Interest income = accrued market discount = $35.
  3. Capital gain = $50 − $35 = $15.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: calling all $50 a capital gain; treating market discount like OID (taxed yearly).

LO 2.2.1 · Convertible bond math

−

A convertible = a straight bond + an option to swap into a fixed number of the issuer's shares at the holder's choice. Lower coupon than straight bonds; less volatile than the stock; usually issued by younger firms; good diversifier (low correlation with straight corporates, moderate with stocks).

🧮 Worked example — all five numbers

$1,000 par, 4% semiannual coupon, 10 years left, straight bonds yield 6%, conversion price $62.50, stock $55, convertible trades at $1,000.

  1. Ratio = $1,000 ÷ $62.50 = 16 shares.
  2. CV = 16 × $55 = $880.
  3. IV: N = 20, I/YR = 3, PMT = $20, FV = $1,000 → PV = $851.23.
  4. Floor = greater of $851.23 and $880 = $880.
  5. Conversion premium = $1,000 − $880 = $120 → $120 ÷ $880 = 13.64%.
  6. Investment premium = $1,000 − $851.23 = $148.77 → ÷ $851.23 = 17.48%.
  7. Downside risk = $148.77 ÷ $1,000 = 14.88%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing downside risk by IV (gives 17.48%); using the conversion price instead of the stock price for CV; annual instead of semiannual discounting ($852.80).
Try it: The stock jumps to $70. New CV and floor? (16 × $70 = $1,120; floor $1,120)
⚖️ Commonly confused — three ratios, three denominators
MeasureNumeratorDenominator
Conversion premiumPrice − CVCV
Investment premiumPrice − IVIV
Downside riskPrice − IVPrice
How to tell: “premium over X” divides by X; “how far could it fall” divides by today's price.
Exam trap: downside risk and investment premium have the same dollars but different percentages. Downside risk always runs to IV, regardless of CV.
Client conversation: “You're paying about 17% more than this bond is worth as a plain bond. That extra buys the chance to share in the stock's upside. If the stock tanks, the bond value is the cushion — roughly a 15% drop from today — though that cushion shifts if rates move.”
🔬 Go deeper — forced conversion+

Conversion is normally the holder's call, but an issuer can force it. If CV is $1,150 and the bond is called at $1,040, the holder converts to keep $1,150 of value; coupons stop. The holder can sell the shares and buy another bond for income.

The textbook notes the bond certificate expresses the conversion terms as a price (the conversion price) rather than a share count; you derive the ratio from it.

LO 2.3.1 · Immunization, ladders, barbells and bullets

−

Immunization sets interest rate (price) risk against reinvestment risk. Rates up: prices fall, but coupons reinvest higher. Rates down: the reverse. Full offset when portfolio duration = liability duration (time horizon). Used mainly by pensions, endowments and insurers; individuals use it for goals like tuition.

  • Zero-coupon bond — duration = maturity, so one zero maturing on the goal date immunizes it.
  • Coupon bond — duration < maturity, so pick a maturity somewhat beyond the horizon.
  • Rebalancing — portfolio duration falls faster than liability duration; immunization is not fully passive.
StructureShapeWhy use itManagement
LadderEqual amounts at staggered maturitiesMore yield than all-short; steady cash to roll to the long endPassive, long term
Barbell (dumbbell)Short + long onlyLong end locks rates; short end frees cash if bonds fallActive
BulletBought over time, all mature on one dateMatch duration to one cash need; limit rate riskPlanned purchases

In both ladders and barbells, short bonds limit losses if rates rise; long bonds offer big gains if rates fall.

🧮 Worked example — building a ladder and a bullet

Ladder: $150,000 split into $30,000 at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years. Bullet: $90,000 needed in 8 years.

  1. Ladder: each year the maturing $30,000 rung buys a new 5-year bond, keeping the 1–5 year shape.
  2. Bullet: buy an 8-year bond now, a 6-year bond in year 2, a 4-year bond in year 4 — all mature in year 8.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: calling short + long only a ladder (it's a barbell); matching maturity rather than duration to the horizon with coupon bonds.
Try it: Horizon 7 years. Which immunizes: a 7-year zero or a 7-year 6% coupon bond? (The zero)
Exam trap: a ladder yields more than an all-short portfolio, and a barbell (not a ladder) is the active one.
Client conversation: “We'll stagger your bonds so one matures every year. If rates rise, the money coming due gets reinvested at the higher rate; if they fall, the longer bonds have already locked in today's rate.”

  1. Loss = $20,000 − $18,000 = $2,000.
  2. Textbook's simplified saving = $2,000 × 0.24 = $480.

LO 2.4.1 · Yield curve shapes and theories

−

A yield curve plots yield against maturity for bonds of the same quality (the term structure of interest rates). The Treasury curve is the most watched. Its shape shifts with the economic cycle, so it hints at what's next.

ShapeLooks likeUsually signals
Normal (positive)Long > short (often ~300 bp gap)Expansion; rates expected to rise
FlatAbout equalEconomy peaking; little rate change expected
Inverted (negative)Short > longFed tightened against inflation; rates expected to fall; possible recession
HumpedMiddle maturities highestNo separate signal given in the textbook
TheoryCore ideaShapes explained
Unbiased expectationsLong rate = geometric mean of current and expected short ratesAny
Liquidity preferenceInvestors need a premium to lend long (volatility, liquidity)Upward only
Market segmentationSupply/demand within maturity segments; institutions match liabilitiesAny
Unbiased expectations: 1 + RN = [(1 + R1)(1 + E(r2)) … (1 + E(rN))]1/N
where R1 = today's one-period rate and E(rt) = the one-period rate expected in period t.

Segments: short (≤1 yr: banks lend, consumer finance firms borrow), intermediate (1–5 yrs: savings and loans lend, manufacturers borrow for working capital), long (>5 yrs: life insurers lend; plant expansion borrows).

🧮 Worked example — two-year rate from expectations

The 1-year rate is 2.5%; next year's 1-year rate is expected at 3.5%.

  1. 1 + R2 = (1.025 × 1.035)1/2 = (1.060875)1/2 = 1.0300.
  2. R2 ≈ 3.00% → expected higher short rates produce an upward curve.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: skipping the root (6.09%); using only the latest expected rate (3.5%).
Try it: 3.0% today, 3.4% and 3.9% expected. 3-year rate? (≈3.43%)
Exam trap: only liquidity preference is limited to an upward slope. Market segmentation in its strong form says institutions never leave their segment.
Client conversation: “Normally you get paid more to lend longer. When short bonds pay more than long ones, the market expects rates to come down — often because the Fed is fighting inflation — and that has sometimes come before a recession.”

1-year bills yield 5.2%; 20-year call-protected bonds yield 4.4%. The client expects the curve to normalize.

  1. Bills pay more today, but mature in a year and get reinvested at lower rates.
  2. The 20-year bond locks 4.4% for two decades and rises in price as rates fall.

LO 2.5.1 · Preferred stock

−

A hybrid: a fixed dividend set as a % of par, but the board must declare it. Paid before common dividends. No maturity (a perpetuity), so price moves inversely with rates — more than the same firm's bonds.

  • Cumulative — skipped preferred dividends (arrears) must be paid before any common dividend.
  • Buyers — investors wanting a higher yield than the common and a steady cash flow.
  • Dividends-received deduction — corporate holders deduct 50% (own <20%) or 65% (own 20%+), within limits (2026 edition figure).
  • Convertible preferred — higher dividend than common, upside via conversion, priority over common; popular for funding venture capital and private equity.
Like bonds
  • Fixed periodic cash flow
  • No vote; no profit sharing
  • Callable/redeemable; may be convertible
  • May have sinking funds
  • Ahead of common in bankruptcy
Unlike bonds
  • Dividends not deductible by issuer
  • DRD applies to dividends, not interest
  • Missed dividend ≠ bankruptcy
  • Quarterly vs semiannual payments
  • Different accounting
No-growth preferred value: V = D ÷ r
D = annual dividend; r = required return (investor) or market yield on similar preferreds
🧮 Worked example — rate sensitivity

$100 par, 4.5% preferred → $4.50 dividend.

  1. Market yield rises to 6%: V = $4.50 ÷ 0.06 = $75 (−25%).
  2. Market yield falls to 4%: V = $4.50 ÷ 0.04 = $112.50 (+12.5%).
⚠️ Common wrong methods: basing the dividend on market price; assuming price stays at par because the dividend is fixed.
Try it: $50 par, 5% preferred, yields rise to 6.25%. ($40, −20%)
Exam trap: the dividend is a % of par, not market price, and preferred pays quarterly.
Client conversation: “Preferreds pay a steady dividend ahead of common shareholders, but they never mature. If rates rise, their price can drop more than a bond's, so they fit best when rates look steady or falling.”
