LO 6.1.1 · Risk, return and diversification

−

Investment risk is the chance that what you actually earn differs from what you expected. The two main tools for managing it are diversification and asset allocation. The goal is the most return per unit of risk, which comes from (1) combining assets with low correlation, (2) a longer time horizon, or both.

  • Expected return — the investor's estimate of return given the economic and market outlook
  • Diversification — holding enough loosely correlated securities that losses in some are offset by gains in others; removes unsystematic risk. Foreign securities help because they are usually not highly correlated with U.S. ones
  • Asset classes — cash & equivalents, fixed income, equities, real assets (real estate, collectibles, precious metals, stones)
  • Hedges — fixed income hedges deflation; equities hedge inflation

Large-cap stocks, different industries10–15 (exam rule)

🧮 Worked example — allocated but not diversified?

Imani, 29, holds 100% common stock: 14 large-cap names spread over 9 industries. Her brother Kofi owns one bank stock, one corporate bond, one money market fund and one rental condo.

  1. Imani: one asset class (no allocation), but 14 large caps across industries meets the 10–15 rule → diversified within equities.
  2. Kofi: four asset classes (allocated), but a single holding in each → not diversified; heavy firm-specific risk.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: treating "owns several asset classes" as proof of diversification, or treating one class as automatically undiversified.
Try it: A client owns 20 small-cap stocks in different industries. Diversified by the textbook rule? (Not yet — small caps need about 25–30.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — asset allocation vs. diversification

How to tell: count classes for allocation; count low-correlation holdings for diversification.
Exam trap: more risk raises the return investors require; it does not guarantee a higher realized return. Judge risk and return together — an 8% choice can beat a 10% one on a risk-adjusted basis.
Client conversation: "Owning stocks, bonds and real estate is a great start, but if each bucket is just one or two names, one bad company can still sink you. We want enough different holdings in each bucket that no single surprise matters much — and the longer your timeline, the more short-term swings you can ride out."
🔬 Go deeper — other views of risk+

Besides loss of principal and return variability, the textbook lists price volatility, failing to earn the expected return, cash flows that don't match fixed living costs, and general uncertainty about future returns. A distorted view of risk leads either to a portfolio riskier than the client can tolerate or to one so cautious (e.g., all money market) that it cannot reach the goal. Perception of risk is often shaped by recent news and stories rather than actual probabilities, which pushes investors to buy after rises and sell after drops.

The risk-return pyramid ranks products from safest (insured deposits, Treasuries, savings bonds) up through high-grade bonds and stocks to speculative stocks, collectibles, limited partnerships, options and futures at the top.

LO 6.1.2 · Sources of risk: systematic vs. unsystematic

−
Total risk = unsystematic (diversifiable) + systematic (nondiversifiable)
Total risk → measured by standard deviation  |  systematic risk → measured by beta

Diversification can largely remove unsystematic risk, so systematic risk is the part investors should worry about most.

Unsystematic (one company, country or sector)

  • Business — uncertain operating income from the firm's (and sector's) practices
  • Financial — debt-vs-equity mix hurts the equity's value
  • Default (credit) — a debt issuer misses interest or principal; junk bonds most exposed; common stock not exposed
  • Political — a country's political/economic climate or law changes hurt the investment
  • Tax — uncertainty in tax law that could change returns
  • Investment manager — a fund manager's skill, philosophy, team or style changes
  • Liquidity — can't sell quickly without significant loss of principal (direct real estate, closely held firms)
  • Marketability — can't find a ready buyer

Systematic — PRIME (whole market)

  • Purchasing power — unexpected inflation; hits bonds, CDs, cash
  • Reinvestment rate — cash flows reinvested at lower rates; long bonds, GNMAs. Zero-coupon bonds eliminate it
  • Interest rate — bond prices move opposite to rates
  • Market — overall market volatility, from tangible events (wars, collapses) or intangible ones (speculation)
  • Exchange rate — currency moves cut a foreign holding's dollar return; Yankee bonds reduce it
🧮 Worked example — exchange rate risk

Theo, in the U.S., owns a Japanese bond paying ¥10,000 interest. At purchase $1 = ¥100; now $1 = ¥125.

  1. Then: ¥10,000 ÷ 100 = $100
  2. Now: ¥10,000 ÷ 125 = $80
  3. The dollar buys more yen, so it strengthened and Theo's dollar income fell $20.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying by the rate, or calling "more yen per dollar" a weaker dollar.
Try it: If the rate moved to $1 = ¥80 instead? (¥10,000 ÷ 80 = $125; the weaker dollar helps Theo by $25.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — business vs. financial; liquidity vs. marketability
PairFirstSecond
Business vs. financialOperations → operating incomeLeverage → capital structure
Liquidity vs. marketabilitySell quickly without a loss?Any buyer at all?
How to tell: ask "what is the source?" — how the firm operates vs. how it is financed; speed/price vs. existence of a market.
Exam trap: liquidity risk is unsystematic even though it sounds market-wide — it is not in PRIME. And a shareholder never faces default risk; a privately held company's shareholders do face liquidity risk.
Client conversation: "Some risks belong to one company — a bad CEO, too much debt. Owning many companies takes care of those. Other risks hit everything at once — inflation, rate moves, a market sell-off. We can't diversify those away, so we size how much of them you carry and keep you focused on the long run when markets get rough."
🔬 Go deeper — devaluation, revaluation and "liquid"+

When a country deliberately lowers its currency's value against another (usually the dollar), that is devaluation; raising it is revaluation. A weakening U.S. dollar benefits U.S. investors who own foreign securities.

"Liquid" is used loosely. Check whether the speaker means safety of principal, access to the money, a small loss on conversion to cash, or a small price concession when selling. Stocks carry some liquidity risk because their price can be below what you paid when you need to sell. Bonds with more default risk may also show more interest rate risk.

LO 6.2.1 · Beta and weighted beta

−

Beta is a relative measure of systematic risk: how volatile an asset is compared with the market (beta = 1.0). It suits diversified portfolios with little unsystematic risk; standard deviation suits every asset.

Beta: βi = COVim ÷ σm2 = (ρim × σi) ÷ σm
where COVim = covariance with the market, σm2 = market variance, ρim = correlation with the market, σ = standard deviation
Weighted (portfolio) beta: βp = Σ (Wi × βi), W = market-value weight
< 1.0
Defensive — less volatile than market
1.0
Moves with the market (index fund)
> 1.0
Aggressive — more volatile
0 / −
No correlation (T-bills) / moves opposite (gold, inverse ETFs)

Example reading: beta 1.30 = 30% more volatile than the market; 0.80 = 20% less. Most well-known stocks fall between 0 and +2, but speculative or distressed stocks can exceed 3.

🧮 Worked example — beta two ways, then a portfolio

Harbor Growth Fund: σ = 24; market σ = 15; correlation 0.70.

  1. Correlation route: 0.70 × 24 ÷ 15 = 1.12
  2. Covariance route: COV = 0.70 × 24 × 15 = 252; 252 ÷ 152 (225) = 1.12
  3. Portfolio: $40,000 at β 1.20, $35,000 at 0.95, $25,000 at 0.60 (total $100,000) → 0.40(1.20) + 0.35(0.95) + 0.25(0.60) = 0.48 + 0.3325 + 0.15 = 0.9625
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing COV by σm (not σm2); forgetting the correlation (24 ÷ 15 = 1.60); simple-averaging the betas instead of dollar-weighting.
Try it: COV with market = 180, market σ = 15. Beta? (180 ÷ 225 = 0.80.)

Why beta can mislead: beta = (σi ÷ σm) × ρ. Hold the volatility ratio at 2 and lower the correlation:

σi ÷ σmρBetaLooks like…
21.002.0Twice as volatile (true)
20.601.2Only 20% more volatile
20.300.6Defensive — but it isn't
Exam trap: a low beta does not mean low volatility. Gold funds often show low betas against the S&P 500 yet swing widely — the low correlation is doing the work. As correlation falls, so does beta's reliability (see R2, LO 6.2.5).
Client conversation: "Beta tells us how hard your portfolio tends to move when the whole market moves. At 1.07 you'd expect a bit more bounce than the market; if that feels like too much, we can blend in steadier holdings to bring the number down."
🔬 Go deeper — negative beta and adding a low-correlation holding+

A negative beta (rare) means the asset tends to move against the market, so it may cushion a diversified portfolio in a bear market. A holding with low correlation can be a good diversifier: it should lower portfolio standard deviation, and it lowers portfolio beta if its own beta is below the portfolio's current beta and the rest of the portfolio is diversified. Calculator: enter each beta, then its dollar amount as the weight with Σ+, and read the weighted mean (HP 10bII+ x̄w).

LO 6.2.2 · Mean, weighted return and standard deviation

−

Standard deviation is an absolute measure of how widely actual returns scatter around the mean — the most widely accepted measure of total risk.

Weighted average return: Rp = Σ (Wi × Ri)
Sample SD (exam default): S = √[ Σ(rt − r̄)2 ÷ (n − 1) ]
Population SD: σ = √[ Σ(rt − r̄)2 ÷ n ]
rt = return in period t; r̄ = mean return; n = number of observations
  • Mean — sum of observations ÷ count; best gauge of central tendency
  • Median — middle value when sorted low to high
  • Mode — most frequent value
🧮 Worked example — weighted return, then SD of five years

Nadia: $60,000 large-cap fund (11%), $25,000 bond fund (6%), $15,000 REIT (4%).

  1. Weighted return = 0.60(11) + 0.25(6) + 0.15(4) = 6.60 + 1.50 + 0.60 = 8.70%

A fund returned 12%, −4%, 9%, 15%, 3%.

  1. Mean = 35 ÷ 5 = 7%
  2. Deviations: 5, −11, 2, 8, −4 → squared: 25, 121, 4, 64, 16 → sum 230
  3. Sample variance = 230 ÷ 4 = 57.5 → S = √57.5 = 7.58%
  4. (Population: 230 ÷ 5 = 46 → σ = 6.78%)
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing by n for historical data; stopping at the variance; simple-averaging returns of unequal-sized holdings.
Try it: Returns 4%, 10%, 16%. Sample SD? (Mean 10; squares 36 + 0 + 36 = 72; 72 ÷ 2 = 36; S = 6%.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — sample vs. population SD
Sample (S)Population (σ)
Divide byn − 1n
Calculator keySxσx
Exam useYes — historical returnsRarely; whole populations are unrealistic
How to tell: a handful of past years is a sample → n − 1.
Exam trap: the textbook calls standard deviation "also known as variance," but variance is the squared figure. If your answer is in squared-percent units (e.g., 57.5), take the square root.
Client conversation: "Your average return tells you where the fund tends to land; the standard deviation tells you how far a typical year wanders from that average. Two funds can both average 7%, but one that wanders 4% either way is a smoother ride than one that wanders 15%."

LO 6.2.2 · Return ranges, z-scores and distribution shape

−

A normal distribution is a symmetrical bell curve: mean = median = mode, and half of outcomes fall on each side of the mean.

68%
within ±1 SD
95%
within ±2 SD
99%
within ±3 SD (beyond = outliers)
Range: mean ± (k × SD), k = 1, 2 or 3
z-statistic: z = (value − mean) ÷ SD   (+ above mean, − below)
🧮 Worked example — ranges and tails

Portfolio mean 8%, SD 6%.

  1. 68%: 8 ± 6 → 2% to 14%
  2. 95%: 8 ± 12 → −4% to 20%
  3. 99%: 8 ± 18 → −10% to 26%
  4. Tail: 95% inside ±2 SD leaves 5% outside, split evenly → about 2.5% of years above 20%
  5. z for a −1% year: (−1 − 8) ÷ 6 = −1.5
⚠️ Common wrong methods: putting the whole 5% (or 32%) in one tail; using 99.7% instead of the textbook's 99%; dropping the sign on z.
Try it: Mean 9%, SD 4%. 99% range? (9 ± 12 → −3% to 21%.)
Shape termWhat it means

Exam trap: the textbook's rule is 68 / 95 / 99. And use lognormal for the portfolio's future value, normal for its yearly returns.
Client conversation: "With an 8% average and 6% standard deviation, about two years in three should land between 2% and 14%, and about nineteen in twenty between −4% and 20%. A losing year is normal — it's inside the range we planned for."
🔬 Go deeper — why critics like semivariance+

Standard deviation treats a surprise gain the same as a surprise loss. A risk-averse investor really cares about the downside, so semivariance looks only at returns below the mean. Behavioral studies cited in the textbook find investors may accept a lower expected return for the chance of a few very large gains (a preference for positive skew).

LO 6.2.3 · Coefficient of variation

−

The coefficient of variation (CV) is relative risk: total risk (standard deviation) per unit of return. Use it to compare investments when both returns and standard deviations differ. Lower is better.

CV = σ ÷ x̄
σ = standard deviation; x̄ = mean (expected) return
🧮 Worked example — three funds
FundMeanSDCV
Alder8%10%10 ÷ 8 = 1.25
Birch12%18%18 ÷ 12 = 1.50
Cedar5%4.5%4.5 ÷ 5 = 0.90
  1. Cedar has the least risk per unit of return (lowest CV).
  2. Birch has the highest return but also the most risk per unit of return.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: flipping the ratio (return ÷ SD); using beta in place of SD; picking the highest CV.
Try it: Return 12%, SD 18%, beta 1.10. CV? (18 ÷ 12 = 1.50 — beta is a distractor.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — CV vs. COV
CVCOV (covariance)
Formulaσ ÷ meanρ × σi × σj
Assets involvedOneTwo
Used forCross-check on an investment choiceBasis of portfolio diversification
How to tell: "per unit of return" → CV; "move together" → COV.
Exam trap: CV questions often pad the facts (beta, R2, alpha). You need only the standard deviation and the mean.
Client conversation: "Fund B earns more, but it makes you ride a much bumpier road for each point of return. Per unit of bumpiness, Fund A pays you better."

LO 6.2.4 · Covariance, correlation and two-asset portfolio risk

−

Covariance shows whether two assets' returns move together (+) or opposite (−), but it has no bounds. The correlation coefficient (R, r or ρ — same number) standardizes it to −1 … +1. Squaring R gives R2.

Covariance: COVij = ρij × σi × σj
Correlation: ρij = COVij ÷ (σi × σj)
Two-asset portfolio SD: σp = √[ Wi2σi2 + Wj2σj2 + 2WiWjCOVij ]
W = weight in each asset (Wi + Wj = 100%)
ρMeaning (market up 10%)
+1Asset up about 10% — no diversification
0No linear link — asset could rise, fall or stay flat
−1Asset down about 10% (rare; e.g., a derivative)
🧮 Worked example — 70/30 bond/stock mix

Bond fund σ = 12 (70%); stock fund σ = 25 (30%); ρ = 0.20.

  1. COV = 0.20 × 12 × 25 = 60
  2. 0.702 × 144 = 70.56; 0.302 × 625 = 56.25; 2 × 0.70 × 0.30 × 60 = 25.20
  3. Sum = 152.01 → σp = √152.01 = 12.33%
  4. Weighted-average SD would be 0.70(12) + 0.30(25) = 15.90% — true only if ρ = +1

⚠️ Common wrong methods: weight-averaging SDs when ρ < 1; plugging ρ where COV belongs; leaving out the "2"; forgetting the square root.
Try it: COV = 96, SDs 12 and 16. Correlation? (96 ÷ 192 = 0.50.)
Exam trap: if ρ is below +1, portfolio SD is below the weighted-average SD. Zero risk is possible only at ρ = −1 with the right weights (e.g., 50/50 when the two SDs are equal).
Building with funds: two funds at +0.88 and +0.93 to the S&P 500 are near-duplicates. Pair a high-correlation fund with a low one (e.g., +0.35). Low positive correlations diversify well; negative ones are not required. If you own no index fund and two funds show low correlations to the S&P 500, you still must check their correlation with each other.
Client conversation: "Adding a fund that zigs when your others zag is what lowers the bumps. These two funds have different names, but they move together almost exactly — owning both is like owning one. Let's swap one for something that marches to a different beat."
🔬 Go deeper — correlations aren't constant+

Correlations differ by the time frame measured, so long-run figures can mislead after a recent shift. Studies show correlations tend to fall in rising markets and rise in falling markets — the opposite of what investors want, since diversification matters most in a decline. Covariance is the most important input in the portfolio formula, more than the weights.

LO 6.2.5 · Coefficient of determination (R2)

−

R2 is the square of the correlation coefficient. It is the share of an asset's movement explained by the benchmark (systematic risk); 1 − R2 comes from other factors (unsystematic risk, often assumed to be mainly manager skill).

R2 = ρ2  |  unsystematic share = 1 − R2
Beta reliable: R2 ≥ 0.70 (Morningstar threshold) ≈ ρ of ±0.84
≥ 0.70
Beta usable; Treynor & Jensen OK
< 0.70
Use SD-based Sharpe, or a better benchmark
≈ 1.00
Likely an index fund
🧮 Worked example — is beta trustworthy here?

Fund σ = 20; market σ = 16; covariance = 224.

  1. ρ = 224 ÷ (20 × 16) = 224 ÷ 320 = 0.70
  2. R2 = 0.702 = 0.49 → 49% explained by the market; 51% by other factors
  3. Beta = 224 ÷ 162 = 0.875 — but with R2 below 0.70, don't lean on it; use Sharpe (standard deviation)
⚠️ Common wrong methods: treating the correlation (0.70) as R2; reporting R2 as the unsystematic share; thinking a high R2 means good diversification.
Try it: ρ = 0.90 vs. ρ = 0.60. R2? (0.81 → beta usable; 0.36 → use Sharpe.)

How to tell: a negative number can only be R; a "percentage explained" is R2.
Exam trap: a fund with R2 above 0.70 against an S&P 500 index fund is a poor diversifier for it — unless the high R2 comes from strong negative correlation (ρ ≈ −0.84), which does diversify. The 0.70 cutoff is gradual, not a cliff.
Client conversation: "This 'active' fund moves 98% in step with the S&P 500 but charges a lot more than an index fund. You're paying for stock picking you're not really getting — the index fund gives you nearly the same ride for less."
🔬 Go deeper — picking a better benchmark+

When R2 against the S&P 500 is low, compute beta against an index the holding actually tracks — a gold index for a gold fund, an emerging-markets index for an emerging-markets fund. Beta is most reliable at R2 = 1.00 and becomes steadily less useful as R2 falls. Treynor and Jensen (beta-based) assume a diversified portfolio; Sharpe (standard-deviation-based) works for any portfolio or stock. These measures are covered in another module.
