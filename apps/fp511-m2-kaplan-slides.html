Learning Objective 2.1.1

Risk tolerance & loss aversion

🎯Matching recommendations to risk propensity

Analyze a client's degrees of risk tolerance and loss aversion, and ensure recommendations are consistent with the client's risk propensity, attitudes, composure, capacity, knowledge, and needs.

The five components of risk (Kaplan)
ComponentWhat it captures
Risk toleranceThe client's willingness to accept uncertainty / volatility
Risk preferenceThe client's desire for, or aversion to, taking risk
Risk perceptionThe client's subjective judgment of how much risk is involved
Risk capacityThe client's financial ability to absorb a loss
Risk literacyThe client's ability to understand and use risk information
Don't blur themWillingness (tolerance/preference) and ability to bear loss (capacity) are different — a recommendation must respect both.

Learning Objective 2.1.2

Psychology, background & learning style

🔎Understanding psychological profiles

A profile helps predict how a client will perceive and judge recommendations. Two building blocks:

Perception
How people become aware of things, people, events, or ideas.
Judgment
Making conclusions about what has been perceived.

Personality assessments place clients along four dichotomies: introverted or extroverted · driven by senses or intuition · influenced by thinking or feeling · likely to perceive or judge.

🎓Three learning styles

How clients take in and retain information
StyleHow the client learns best
VisualResponds to visual objects — graphs, charts, and pictures
AuditoryRetains information by hearing or speaking
KinestheticUnderstands concepts using a hands-on approach; enjoys physical activity and expresses with body language
Exam cueGraphs, charts & pictures = visual. Physical activity & body language = kinesthetic. (See the Learning Styles question at the end.)

Learning Objective 2.2.1

Cognitive & emotional biases

🧠Cognitive vs. emotional — the master distinction

Cognitive biases (errors) come from faulty reasoning, information-processing mistakes, or a lack of proper statistical analysis — decision-making based on well-known concepts that may or may not be correct. Emotional biases are not related to conscious thought; they arise from feelings, impulses, or intuition.

📋The 13 cognitive biases (errors)

Cognitive biases named in the module
BiasShort description
Illusion of controlBelieving you can control or influence outcomes you actually can't
Money illusionFocusing on nominal dollars rather than real, inflation-adjusted value
ConservatismClinging to prior views and under-reacting to new information
HindsightSeeing past events as having been predictable ("I knew it all along")
ConfirmationSeeking information that confirms existing beliefs, ignoring the rest
RepresentativenessJudging by stereotypes or how well something fits a category
Mental accountingTreating money differently based on its source or intended purpose
Cognitive dissonanceDiscomfort from conflicting beliefs; rationalizing to reduce it
Self-attributionCrediting successes to skill but blaming failures on bad luck
AnchoringOver-relying on an initial reference point when deciding
OutcomeJudging a decision by its result rather than the quality of the decision
FramingDecisions swayed by how the information is presented
RecencyOverweighting recent events or experiences

❤️The 7 emotional biases

Emotional biases named in the module
BiasShort description
Loss aversionLosses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good
OverconfidenceOverestimating one's own knowledge or abilities
Self-controlFavoring short-term gratification over long-term goals
Status quoPreferring things stay the same — inertia / doing nothing

Exam trap — endowment vs. status quoEndowment = won't sell because the asset feels special because it's owned (e.g., inherited, sentimental stock). Status quo = won't sell simply to avoid changing anything. (See the Emotional Biases question below.)

Learning Objective 2.2.2

Money scripts, comfort zones & disorders

📜What drives day-to-day money behavior

A client's psychology — their financial comfort zone, socialization, money beliefs, and past experiences — shapes their objectives, decision-making, and actions. Two core ideas:

Money scripts
A client's underlying money beliefs, often formed early and largely unconscious.
Financial comfort zones
The range of financial behavior a client feels at ease with.

⚠️Five money disorders

Disordered money behaviors named in the module
DisorderShort description
Gambling disorderCompulsive gambling despite harmful consequences
Compulsive buyingAn uncontrollable urge to shop and spend
Financial dependence ("affluenza")Reliance on money one did not earn
Financial enablingGiving money in a way that harms the recipient; difficulty saying no
Hoarding disorderPersistent difficulty parting with possessions

Financial transparency with family

🔦Why transparency matters

  • Fosters honesty and trust in relationships
  • Spouses and family members must be clear and unambiguous about financial matters
  • A lack of transparency can sabotage attaining shared goals
  • If partners' goals differ, at the very least each should respect the other's goals

Learning Objective 2.3.5

Money as undue influence & abuse

⛔Three forms of financial manipulation

Situations where money is used for undue influence, control, or abuse — power imbalances, financial abuse, and financial enabling:

Financial control
One party dominates financial decisions to hold power over another.
Financial enabling
(financial enmeshment)
Blurred boundaries; providing money in ways that foster unhealthy dependence.
Financial abuse
Using money to control, coerce, or harm another person.

Learning Objective 2.4.2

Building honesty & trust

🌍Cultural competence vs. cultural humility

To develop a relationship of honesty and trust, planners should first understand their own identities, then bring:

Cultural competence
Knowledge and skill to work effectively across cultures.
Cultural humility
Ongoing self-reflection and openness — recognizing you can never fully "master" another's culture.
Start with selfTrust begins when planners understand their own identity and biases before judging the client's.

Kaplan questions from this module

Learning styles
Brynlee has a kinesthetic learning style. Which statements about Brynlee are CORRECT?
I. She is likely to enjoy physical activities. II. She expresses herself with body language. III. Graphs, charts, and pictures are useful in presenting information to her.
A. II only · B. III only · C. I and II · D. I, II, and III
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — I and II

A kinesthetic learner enjoys physical activities and expresses herself with body language. Statement III (graphs, charts, and pictures) describes a visual learning style, not kinesthetic.

Emotional biases
Phan inherited ABC stock from his late father, who bought it the day Phan was born. His planner recommends selling it, but Phan won't because it is sentimental. This best describes which emotional bias?
A. Status quo bias · B. Overconfidence · C. Endowment bias · D. Regret-aversion bias
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Endowment bias

Endowment bias occurs when an asset is considered special and more valuable simply because it is already owned.

Financial conflict
Which statement regarding financial conflict is CORRECT?
A. Nonverbal behaviors are not as important as spoken words · B. Emphasize what partners discuss over how they discuss it · C. Use general conversations about expectations for adult children · D. If partners have comparable retirement balances, each should invest per their own risk tolerance
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D — each partner invests per their own risk tolerance

With comparable retirement balances, it's often good strategy for each partner to invest per their own risk tolerance. Nonverbal behavior is equally important as the spoken word (A wrong), planners give equal weight to what and how partners say things (B wrong), and adult-child conflict is best eased with realistic, preferably written guidelines — not just general conversations (C wrong).
