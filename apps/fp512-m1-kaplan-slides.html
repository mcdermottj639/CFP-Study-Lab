Learning Objective 1.1.1

Concepts of risk — terms & classifications

🗂️Three ways to classify risk

Risk is sorted along three tested pairings:

Static vs. dynamic
Static risks occur in a stable economy (e.g. fire, theft); dynamic risks arise from economic change.
Fundamental vs. particular
Fundamental risks affect groups/society; particular risks affect specific individuals.
Pure vs. speculative
Pure risk = chance of loss or no loss (insurable); speculative risk = chance of loss or gain (not insurable).
Insurability ruleInsurance handles pure risk only — where the outcome is loss or no loss. Speculative risk (a chance of gain) is not insurable.

🔥Peril vs. hazard — don't confuse them

Two words the exam loves to swap
TermWhat it isExamples
PerilThe cause of a lossFire, flood, storm, collision
HazardA condition that increases the chance or severity of a lossAn oily rag left in a garage
Exam trapA fire, flood, or storm that destroys property is a peril, not a hazard. The hazard is the underlying condition (the oily rag) that makes the loss more likely.

Handling risk — control, financing & the matrix

🛠️Risk control vs. risk financing

The four methods of addressing risk
CategoryMethodWhat it means
Risk controlAvoidanceEliminate the exposure entirely
ReductionLower the frequency or severity of loss
Risk financingRetentionKeep the risk / pay losses yourself
TransferShift the risk to an insurer

🎯The risk-management matrix

Match the technique to loss frequency (how often) and severity (how costly):

Anchor exampleA brand-new luxury sedan's collision exposure = high severity / low probability → Transfer the risk to an insurer.

🏦Self-insurance — a form of retention

Self-insurance is not true insurance (no transfer) — it's a formal way of retaining risk. To do it properly requires:

  • Enough homogeneous exposure units
  • Adequate funds to cover losses
  • Ability to administer insurance functions
  • Competent management of the self-insurance fund's investments
Exam trapSelf-insurance is retention, NOT transfer — even though it "feels" like being your own insurer.

📐Elements of an insurable risk

An insurer relies on the law of large numbers: enough homogeneous exposure units to make losses reasonably predictable. Beyond that, the loss produced must be:

Definite & measurable
Loss can be determined and quantified.
Fortuitous / accidental
Unexpected and outside the insured's control.
Not catastrophic
Not ruinous to the insurance company.

🔁The client's life cycle

Asset accumulation
Building wealth — early/mid career.
Conservation / protection
Protecting accumulated assets.
Distribution / gifting
Spending down and transferring wealth.

Learning Objective 1.3.1

Agents, brokers & agency authority

🧑‍💼Agent vs. broker — whom do they represent?

Legal obligations in the insurance agency relationship
FunctionAgentBroker
RepresentsThe insurer (principal)The insured
Authority to bind the insurerExists → binds the insurerDoes not exist → binds only self
Legal obligation createdFor the principal / insurerFor the broker themselves
One-linerAn agent acts for the insurer; a broker acts for the insured. Only the agent can bind the insurance company.

🔑Three types of agency authority

Express
Powers explicitly granted to the agent by the insurer.
Implied
Powers reasonably necessary to carry out express authority.
Apparent (ostensible)
What the public reasonably believes the agent can do.
Exam trap — apparent authorityEven if an insurer privately instructed its agent not to write a policy, the company is bound if the public reasonably believed the agent had authority. Apparent (ostensible) authority still binds the insurer.

Learning Objective 1.5.1

Insurance contract law

📜Special legal characteristics of insurance contracts

Aleatory
Parties exchange unequal value (premium vs. potential benefit).
Adhesion
"Take it or leave it" — drafted by the insurer; ambiguities favor the insured.
Conditional
Benefits owed only if conditions are met.
Indemnity
Restores the insured to pre-loss position — no profit.
Personal
Insures the person/interest, not just the property.
Unilateral
Only the insurer makes an enforceable promise.
Utmost good faith
Both parties rely on each other's honesty & full disclosure.
Why "aleatory" is testedBecause premiums & benefits are of unequal value, insurance is aleatory — the outcome depends on a chance event.

✅Legal requirements for an enforceable contract

  • Offer and acceptance
  • Consideration
  • Legal object
  • Competent parties
  • Legal form
Exam trapThe applicant must be a competent party. A contract entered into by a minor is still valid — but voidable by the minor only. Parties do not have to exchange equal value (insurance is aleatory), and the applicant has no right to alter the contract's provisions.

⚖️Waiver vs. estoppel

Two doctrines that limit a party's rights
DoctrineWhat happens
WaiverA party, by their own actions, voluntarily relinquishes a known right.
EstoppelPrevents a party from asserting a known right because another relied on their conduct to their detriment.

Learning Objective 1.6.1

Liability, torts & damages

🚔Criminal vs. tortious behavior

Class of wrong, nature & redress
ClassNature of wrongType of redress
PublicA crime — violation of criminal lawJail term and/or fines
PrivateA tort — infringement of an individual's rights (civil law)Monetary damages

Liability insurance protects against tort (private wrong) liability — where one person infringes on another's rights and can be sued for damages.

🧩Types of torts & the elements of negligence

Torts come in three flavors: intentional, negligence (failure to act prudently — unintentional), and vicarious (liability for a tort committed by someone else).

The four elements of negligence + its defenses
Four elements of negligenceDefenses to negligence
Duty owedAssumption of risk
Breach of dutyContributory negligence
Damages or lossesComparative negligence
Proximate cause—

Strict liability: a party is held liable for damages from their actions or products whether or not at fault.

💰Three types of damages

Special
Specific, measurable out-of-pocket losses (e.g. medical bills, repair costs).
General
Non-economic losses (e.g. pain and suffering).
Punitive
Extra damages to punish reckless or intentional wrongdoing.
Exam trap — punitive damagesPunitive damages are NOT insurable. They exist to punish a wrongdoer who acted recklessly or intentionally — so an insurer will not cover them.

Underwriting & loss adjustment

🧮Underwriting — evaluating the applicant's risk

Underwriters evaluate an applicant's risk; the insurer decides how much coverage to provide and at what price. Three possible results:

Issued as applied for
Standard acceptance.
Issued with modifications
Rated / altered terms.
Declined
Coverage refused.

🔍Core principles of risk & insurance

Four principles to know cold
PrincipleMeaning
Adverse selectionPeople with the highest risk are most likely to buy insurance
Insurable interestThe insured must stand to incur a loss
DeductibleAmount the insured pays before the insurer pays
ExclusionsPerils the policy does not cover
Insurable interest — timingLife insurance: interest need only exist when the policy is issued. Property & casualty: interest must exist when the loss is claimed. You can insure your own home, your spouse's life, and your adult son's boat — but not your neighbor's home.

🤝Loss adjustment — duties of each party

What the insured vs. the insurer must do at claim time
Insured's dutiesInsurer's duties
Provide notice of lossInvestigate
Protect the propertyRepair, replace, or declare a total loss
Cooperate with the insurerApply the "pair or set" clause

Kaplan questions from this module

Hazard vs. peril
Which of the following can most properly be described as a hazard?
A. A fire damages your roof · B. A flood knocks a tree onto your boat · C. A storm destroys your barbeque & lemon trees · D. An oily rag left in your garage catches fire and destroys your home
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D — the oily rag left in the garage

The oily rag is a hazard — a condition that increases the chance of loss. Fire, flood, and storm are perils (the cause of loss), not hazards.

RM process
Once you have identified and established risk management goals, what is the next step in the risk management process?
A. Develop a risk management plan · B. Implement the recommendations · C. Gather pertinent data to determine risk exposures · D. Analyze & evaluate the information
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Gather pertinent data to determine risk exposures

Step 2 is to gather data. The order is: 1) identify/establish goals, 2) gather data, 3) analyze/evaluate, 4) develop plan, 5) communicate, 6) implement, 7) monitor.

Handling risk
Linda just bought a brand-new luxury sedan. Which risk-management technique best handles the collision exposure?
A. Retention · B. Avoidance · C. Transfer · D. Risk diversification
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Transfer

The collision exposure is high severity / low probability, so transferring it to an insurance company is the most appropriate choice.

Agency authority
Daryl, an AIC agent, was told to stop writing policies but bound a fire policy anyway; the insured's day-care center burned a month later. What is AIC's obligation?
A. Pay — Daryl had express authority · B. Pay — Daryl had ostensible (apparent) authority · C. Not pay — Daryl is the insured's agent · D. Not pay — Daryl failed to disclose
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — apparent (ostensible) authority binds AIC

The public reasonably believed Daryl could write such policies. He is the insurer's agent (not the insured's), so the company is bound by his acts under apparent authority.

Regulation
Which of these provides direct regulation of the insurance industry?
A. The NAIC · B. The IRS · C. Dodd-Frank · D. State departments of insurance
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D — State departments of insurance

State departments regulate insurers directly. The NAIC only issues recommendations; the IRS and Dodd-Frank regulate only indirectly.

Contract law
Which of these is a legal requirement for an enforceable insurance contract?
A. Parties give up goods of equal value · B. Applicant may alter provisions · C. Applicant must be a competent party · D. Insured must be of majority age
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — the applicant must be a competent party

Insurance is aleatory (unequal value), so equal value isn't required. A minor's contract is valid but voidable by the minor only, and the applicant can't alter provisions.

Damages

✓ C — Punitive damages

Punitive damages punish a wrongdoer who acted recklessly or intentionally. They are not insurable.

Insurable interest
Which statements about insurable interest are CORRECT?

Life insurance requires insurable interest only at policy issue; property & casualty requires it only when the loss is claimed.

Joanna has an insurable interest in her own home, her husband's life, and her adult son's boat — but not her neighbor's home.

Agent selection
Which of these is NOT something you'd look for when selecting an insurance agent?
A. Competence · B. Financial strength · C. Inclination to service · D. Education

✓ B — Financial strength

Financial strength is something you look for in an insurance company. In an agent you want competence, inclination to service, experience, training, education/specialization, and reputation.
