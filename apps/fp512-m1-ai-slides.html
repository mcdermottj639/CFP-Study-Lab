Risk fundamentals

The anatomy & nature of risk

🧩The anatomy of a loss

Four linked terms the exam constantly tests. Read the chain left to right:

  • Hazard — a condition that increases the potential for loss (e.g., oily rags next to a heater)
  • Peril — the direct cause of the loss (e.g., fire, flood, hurricane)
  • Risk — the possibility of a loss, or a negative deviation from a desired outcome
  • Loss — the actual financial or physical damage suffered
Exam trap · Peril vs. HazardNever confuse the two. The peril causes the loss; the hazard increases the chance of the peril happening.

🗂️Categorizing the nature of risk

Risk is sorted three ways — by its source of change, its scope of impact, and its potential for gain. Only pure risk is insurable.

Three lenses for classifying risk
LensTypeMeaning & exampleInsurable?
Source of changeStaticOccurs regularly, unrelated to economic shifts (e.g., earthquakes)Yes
DynamicCaused by economic changes (e.g., inflation, recessions)No
Scope of impactFundamentalAffects large groups or society (e.g., war, severe recessions)Generally not
ParticularAffects individuals or small, specific groups (e.g., a single house fire)Yes
Potential for gainPureOnly the chance of loss or no loss — no chance of gainInsurable
SpeculativeChance of loss AND gain (e.g., gambling, investing)Uninsurable
RememberPure risk (loss or no-loss only) is the insurable one. Speculative risk — where gain is possible — is not insurable.

Insurability & underwriting

What can be insured — and how it's priced

✅The elements of an insurable risk

Actuaries price risk on predictable losses. From the insurer's perspective, a risk must meet these criteria:

Criteria an insurer requires before writing a risk
CriterionWhat it means
Definite & MeasurableThe financial loss must be quantifiable to set limits and pay claims
Law of Large NumbersMust have a sufficiently large number of homogeneous exposure units to make losses predictable
FortuitousThe loss must be accidental — not planned or intentional (e.g., arson is excluded)
Non-CatastrophicCannot threaten the solvency of the insurance company itself (e.g., a widespread nuclear disaster)
Note · AI deck quirkThe AI slide's left column labeled two criteria “Definite & Measurable” — a duplicate. The four distinct insurable-risk elements are those listed above (Definite & Measurable, Law of Large Numbers, Fortuitous, Non-Catastrophic). Verify against your course text.

🚪The underwriting gateway

Underwriting's core goal is to control adverse selection — the tendency for high-risk individuals to seek insurance more than average-risk individuals.

Mortality
Incidence of death — drives life insurance premiums.
Morbidity
Incidence of illness/injury — drives disability & health premiums.
Adverse selection
Why underwriting exists — high-risk applicants self-select in.
Exam trap · Moral vs. Morale hazardMoral hazard = unethical intent or misrepresentation to induce a claim (e.g., arson, hiding a DUI). Morale hazard = apathetic indifference because insurance exists (e.g., leaving a car unlocked with keys inside).

Exam trap · Agent vs. BrokerAn agent represents the insurer — legally binds the principal (insurer), and the agent's knowledge is imputed to the insurer. A broker represents the insured (client) and cannot legally bind the insurer.

Deck note: the "Surplus-Line" tile reads "insurers for uninsurable risks." In practice surplus-line carriers cover hard-to-place / non-admitted risks — the standard admitted market declined them, not that they are truly uninsurable. Verify wording with your text.

🏛️The three tiers of agent authority

Express (the foundation)
Explicitly written in the agent's contract with the insurer. Example: written authority to write up to $1M in coverage.
Implied (the structure)
Not expressly written, but reasonably necessary to carry out expected duties; from the insurer–agent relationship. Example: authority to collect premiums.
Apparent (the facade)
From the client–agent relationship; the client reasonably believes authority exists based on actions/materials. Example: a terminated agent who still has company applications and binds a policy.
Test tip · ratificationIf an insurer accepts premiums on a prohibited risk, it has “ratified” the agent's act, legally modifying the agency agreement.

Regulation

The regulatory ecosystem

⚖️Who regulates insurance

State regulation (primary)
Backed by Paul v. Virginia. Operates via the Legislature (laws), Commissioner (executive enforcement), and Courts (interpretation).
Federal regulation (indirect)
Triggered by U.S. v. SEUA, leading to the McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945), which reserved most insurance regulation for the states — leaving federal control only for antitrust and labor laws.
The NAIC
National Association of Insurance Commissioners — all 50 state commissioners. Fosters coordination and introduces model laws for accreditation, but has no legal authority to force state adoption.

Contract law

Building & enforcing the insurance contract

🧷Legal requirements of an enforceable contract

Five pieces must interlock (the exam's "puzzle"):

The five elements of a valid contract
#ElementMeaning
1Offer & AcceptanceInsurer offers to insure; client acknowledges and accepts
2ConsiderationThe exchange of value — insured pays a premium; insurer binds coverage
3Legal ObjectThe contract purpose cannot be illegal (e.g., insuring a counterfeiting operation is unenforceable)
4Competent PartiesParties must be legally capable; minors can enter contracts but the contract is voidable by the minor
5Legal FormMust meet state-prescribed formatting, usually a written document
Test tip · minorsWhile a minor can void a contract, the insurer is generally bound. Competency primarily protects the minor.

📜Unique characteristics of insurance contracts

The five distinguishing characteristics
CharacteristicWhat it means
AleatoryControlled by chance; dollars changing hands are substantially unequal (e.g., $1,000 premium for a $1M payout)
AdhesionPrepared by one party (insurer) with no negotiation — "take it or leave it"
ConditionalThe insurer only pays if a covered loss occurs and specific duties are met
UnilateralOnly one party (the insurer) makes a legally enforceable promise; the insurer cannot sue the client for unpaid premiums
PersonalTies the risk to the individual; cannot be transferred without written permission
Exam trap · utmost good faithUtmost Good Faith is required. A material “Misrepresentation” or “Concealment” can void the contract.

🛠️Resolving contract disputes

Waiver & Estoppel often work as a chain reaction:

  • Waiver — voluntary relinquishment of a known right (e.g., an agent accepts an application that fails criteria)
  • Estoppel — a legal block preventing a party from asserting a right because of their prior actions (e.g., the insurer is estopped from denying the claim because the agent waived the criteria)

Equitable remedies (the court's fix):

Rescission
Contract is deemed null from the beginning due to fraud, concealment, or mutual mistake. Premiums are returned.
Reformation
The written document is rewritten to express the parties' original true intentions (e.g., fixing a misprinted $1M benefit to the intended $100k).

Indemnity & liability

Indemnity, torts & civil liability

🎯Indemnity & insurer liability limits

The Principle of Indemnity: make the insured whole, not wealthy. Four mechanisms enforce it:

Actual Cash Value (ACV)
Replacement Cost minus Depreciation.
Deductibles & Coinsurance
Risk-retention mechanisms that share costs and discourage morale hazard.
Insurable Interest
Must suffer financial/emotional loss. Property: at time of issue AND loss. Life: ONLY at time of issue.
Subrogation
Insurer pays the claim, then assumes the right to sue the at-fault third party to recoup costs — prevents double recovery.
Exam trap · insurable interest timingFor property, insurable interest must exist at issue and at the time of loss. For life, it need only exist at the time of issue.
The exception · Collateral Source RuleIf others cause your loss, they cannot reduce their liability simply because you have insurance.

⚖️The anatomy of a tort & civil liability

The four elements of negligence (an unintentional tort) — all must be present:

The four elements of negligence
#ElementMeaning
1Duty OwedAn obligation to act safely
2Duty BreachedFailure to meet that obligation
3Actual DamagesPhysical, emotional, or financial harm
4Proximate CauseAn uninterrupted sequence of events linking the breach to the damages

Specialized liability concepts:

Attractive Nuisance
A property feature likely to attract/injure children (e.g., an unfenced pool); requires a high degree of care.
Negligence Per Se
Duty of care established by statute (e.g., speeding in a school zone).
Absolute / Strict Liability
Liability without fault for extra-hazardous activities (e.g., keeping wild animals).
Vicarious Liability
Held liable for the acts of another (e.g., a parent for a child, an employer for an employee).

Insurer settlement options:

Replacement
Repair/replace with like kind & quality instead of paying ACV.
Abandonment & Salvage
Insurer takes ownership of totaled property and sells it to reduce losses. The insured cannot force abandonment.
Pair or Set Option
Repair part of the set, or pay the difference in ACV before and after the loss (losing one earring is not a total loss of the set).

A hazard is a condition that increases the potential for loss. The peril would be the resulting fire; the hazard just raises its odds.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Pure risk

Pure risk offers only the chance of loss or no loss — no chance of gain — so it is insurable. Speculative and dynamic risks are not, and fundamental risk is generally uninsurable.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — Life insurance

Life requires insurable interest only at issue. Property requires it at the time of issue AND at the time of loss.

Negligence defenses
Under which defense does being just 1% at fault completely bar the injured party's recovery?
A. Comparative negligence · B. Assumption of risk · C. Contributory negligence · D. Last clear chance
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Contributory negligence

Contributory negligence is the strictest rule — 1% fault bars all recovery. Comparative negligence instead reduces the award proportionally. Last Clear Chance is a modifier that can restore recovery.

Regulation
The McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945) is best described as:
A. Making insurance a federal function · B. Reserving most insurance regulation to the states · C. Creating the NAIC with binding authority · D. Overturning Paul v. Virginia
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — Reserved most regulation to the states

Following U.S. v. SEUA, McCarran-Ferguson left insurance regulation primarily to the states, with federal reach only over antitrust and labor. The NAIC coordinates but cannot force adoption.

Contract characteristics
A $1,000 premium that may pay a $1M benefit reflects which characteristic of insurance contracts?
A. Adhesion · B. Unilateral · C. Aleatory · D. Conditional
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — Aleatory

Aleatory contracts are controlled by chance, with substantially unequal dollar exchange. Adhesion = take-it-or-leave-it; unilateral = only the insurer's promise is enforceable; conditional = pays only if duties are met.
