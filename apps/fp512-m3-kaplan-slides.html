Learning Objective 3.1

Why life insurance — and what to assume

🎯The job of life insurance

The purpose is to provide a death benefit. Common client goals:

  • Pay off debt (mortgage, loans, cards)
  • Replace lost income
  • Fund college education costs
  • Cover final expenses
Exam trapFinal expenses = funeral, last-illness costs, emergency fund, estate taxes. Investment funds are NOT a final expense — they're an asset.

⚖️Economic assumptions vs. client attributes

A classic tested distinction. Only two inputs are broad economic assumptions; the rest describe the client.

What must be assumed vs. what is a client fact
Broad economic assumptionsClient attributes / inputs
Rate of return on investmentsRisk tolerance
Inflation rate for the periodCurrent resources available
RememberRate of return & inflation are the two economic assumptions. Risk tolerance and current resources matter — but they're client attributes, not assumptions.

Learning Objective 3.2

The product ladder

⏳Term life — pure protection

  • Coverage for a specific period; level death benefit (unless decreasing term)
  • Typically annually renewable after the initial term
  • Convertible to permanent; riders customize it
Application — "Darla"Healthy now, family history of mid-life illness, little cash, wants a future permanent option with no new medical exam → Convertible term.

🏛️Whole life — guarantees that don't change

Permanent coverage for the insured's whole life, with three guarantees:

  • Level premiums — never increase
  • Level death benefit
  • Guaranteed cash values (tax-deferred growth; dividends can accelerate it)

Limited pay: higher premiums for a set number of years, then paid-up — no more premium due.

🎚️Universal life — the unbundled cousin

UL unbundles the pieces: cost of insurance, policy expenses, and interest are shown separately. Flexible premiums and an adjustable death benefit. A current and a guaranteed interest rate; may also be indexed.

Exam trapUL does NOT lend itself to a compulsory savings program — flexible premiums let owners underpay, and it can become expensive term.

📊Variable, VUL & EIUL — who bears the risk

Variable life: permanent, guaranteed death benefit, but cash value sits in subaccounts — the owner bears investment risk. VUL combines UL's flexibility with variable subaccounts. EIUL credits a minimum fixed rate in down markets plus limited index participation (with participation & rate caps) in up markets.

Subaccounts ≠ mutual funds
Similar idea, different structure, fees & performance; no need for internal tax efficiency.
EIUL two accounts
Guaranteed interest account + variable indexed account.
EIUL crediting methods
% change · ratchet/point-to-point · spread · high-water mark.

Learning Objective 3.2.5

MEC & how life insurance is taxed

📄Modified Endowment Contract (MEC)

A policy that's overfunded — fails the 7-pay test (deposits in the first 7 years exceed 7 annual net level premiums).

7-pay
Test that triggers MEC status
LIFO
Loans/withdrawals taxed gains-first
10%
Penalty on gain before age 59½

The death benefit keeps its normal tax status — it's living access (loans/withdrawals) that's penalized.

Which policies can become a MEC?Whole life, UL, and VUL — NOT term. Term has no cash-value/investment capacity, so it can never be a MEC.

Learning Objective 3.3

Provisions, options & riders

📌Common contractual provisions

  • Grace period — time to pay a late premium before lapse
  • Incontestability clause — insurer can't contest after a set period
  • Suicide clause — exclusion window early in the policy
  • Reinstatement clause — restore a lapsed policy under conditions
  • Misstatement of age/gender — benefit adjusted to what the premium would have bought

🎯Beneficiary designations

Primary vs. contingent
Primary is paid first; contingent (secondary) only if no primary survives.
Revocable vs. irrevocable
Revocable can be changed anytime; irrevocable needs the beneficiary's consent.
No class limit
There's no limit to the number of beneficiaries in a class.
Exam trapMost beneficiary designations are REVOCABLE — only a small percentage are irrevocable. (Spouses are the most commonly named primary beneficiaries.)

Learning Objective 3.4

How much? Sizing the need

🧮Three methods + the LIFE framework

Needs-determination methods
MethodHow it worksWatch-out
Multiple of salarySalary × number of years (rule of thumb)Simple but imprecise
Human life valuePV of future earnings, discountedCapital is depleted; income-only
Income replacementCapital utilization (spend principal) or capital retention (live on interest, keep principal)Retention needs a larger sum

LIFE totals it up: Liabilities · Income replacement · Final expenses · Education → minus existing insurance = amount needed.

🎓Worked example — education funding (3-step)

Wayne wants insurance to fund college for son Mark (age 4; starts at 18, four-year program). Tuition today $25,000, college inflation 6%, funds earn 8%.

Serial-payment / PVAD method — BEGIN mode

1
Inflate today's cost to the first year of college (14 years out).
$25,000 → 14 N, 6 I/YR → FV = $56,522.60
2
PV of the 4-year serial payment at the inflation-adjusted rate.
[(1.08 ÷ 1.06) − 1] × 100 = 1.8868 I/YR · 4 N · PMT $56,522.60 (BEG) → PV = $219,887.28
3
Discount that lump sum back to today (14 years) at 8%.
$219,887.28 FV, 8 I/YR, 14 N → PV
Include ≈ $74,863.05 in Wayne's life-insurance need for education.

Learning Objective 3.5

Recommend, keep-or-replace & 1035

🔎Keep or replace a policy — factors

  • New acquisition costs (front-end load again)
  • Existing policy values you'd give up
  • New contestability & suicide-clause periods restart
  • Dividends in a new policy are likely lower initially
  • New initial cash values are likely lower
  • A replacement may simply not meet the client's needs
Rule of thumbReplacing term via its own conversion clause is usually the best, least-expensive path. Replacing a cash-value policy with a similar one is seldom advantageous once a policy has been in force.
