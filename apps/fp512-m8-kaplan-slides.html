Learning Objective 8.1.1

Insurance needs grow with business size

📈What drives the premium

As a business grows, its risk exposure grows — and so does the cost and breadth of coverage. Premiums depend on three factors:

👥
Number of employees — more workers' comp, employee liability & PPACA health obligations
💵
Revenue & business operations — more complexity, broader scope of risk
⚠️
Industry risk exposure — construction, manufacturing, etc. carry higher costs

🏢Coverage by size — the ladder

How business insurance needs scale
Business sizeTypical coverage needs
SmallBasic coverage like general liability to protect against common risks; may need a business owner's policy (BOP) bundling general liability with property insurance
MediumAdded complexity & more employees → workers' compensation, professional liability, and health coverage
LargeComprehensive coverage: directors & officers (D&O), extensive property and liability policies, cyber, plus customized industry-specific policies
Key ideaEven small businesses need more than liability alone — property damage and employee injury also require coverage. Large businesses can't simply self-insure: self-insuring can be costly and unpredictable.

Learning Objective 8.1.2

Conducting a business risk assessment

🧭Three fundamental steps

1 · Risk identification
Identify potential threats that could harm the business — financial, operational, legal, and environmental risks.
2 · Risk assessment
Evaluate and prioritize the identified risks by likelihood, potential impact, and need for immediate attention.
3 · Risk control
Implement strategies to prevent or mitigate risk — insurance coverage, safety protocols, or contingency plans.
Order mattersIdentify → assess → control. The owner must stay actively involved — they hold key insights into the business's unique risks and should collaborate with the agent so nothing is overlooked.

🗂️Building a unique risk profile

The assessment feeds a tailored risk profile for the business:

  • Conduct a risk assessment
  • Analyze the information
  • Identify and categorize risks
  • Quantify potential losses
  • Build a unique risk profile
Exam trapAssessments are not a once-every-five-years exercise. Risks change rapidly with market conditions, technology, and regulation — review regularly. Focusing only on financial risk ignores critical threats like employee safety.

Learning Objective 8.1.3

Selecting the company, agent & policy

🏦Optimizing the insurer

Review the company's rating, which is based on:

  • Cash in reserve
  • Debt-to-assets ratio
  • Ethics and risk management
  • Revenue streams
  • Quality of policies underwritten

Then weigh overall service quality: coverage (amount of protection), claims expertise (how well it settles), price (vs. competitors), licensing (not every company is licensed in every state — do due diligence), and reputation (peer experiences).

⭐Insurance-company financial-strength ratings

Memory hookOnly Moody's uses the lowercase-style Aaa … C scale (and bottoms out at C, not D). A.M. Best is the one that tops out at A++; S&P and Fitch both run AAA … D.

🧑‍💼Optimizing the agent & the policy

Industry expertise
The agent should have experience in the specific industry needed.
Reputation & reviews
No disciplinary history; positive reviews; strong claims track record.
Communication
Accessible, responsive, and clear in explaining options and coverage.
Licensing & credentials
Properly licensed with relevant, current certifications.
Cost ≠ valueA low price may signal inadequate coverage; high premiums don't guarantee better protection. Prioritize coverage, reliability, price, and budget so comparisons are meaningful — then a thorough review.

Learning Objective 8.1.4

Funding the business's insurance needs

💰Managing premium costs

Bundle coverage
Combine policies for package pricing.
Increase deductibles
Retain more risk to lower premiums.
Compare quotes
Shop the market before renewing.
Join professional associations
Access group / pooled rates.
Reduce risk
Implement risk-reduction strategies.
Accounting consultants
Regularly review coverage & optimize spend.
Fund it wiselyHire an experienced accountant to manage the books, capture tax credits/deductions, and set the optimal premium spend. Never allocate 100% of profits to insurance, insure only high-risk areas, or commingle personal funds (e.g., a home-equity draw) with the business.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑The business advisory board

A business advisory board can deliver several insurance-related benefits:

  • Expert risk assessment — members bring risk-management expertise
  • Strategic guidance — shape the insurance-portfolio plan
  • Negotiation leverage — experienced pros boost reputation & credibility
  • Compliance & regulatory advice — legal experts on requirements
  • Crisis-management support — help handling a major claim or incident

Typical costs: compensation (a fixed honorarium per meeting, or equity in startups), meeting costs (venue, catering, travel reimbursement, technology), and administrative costs (staff time, legal contract review).

Learning Objective 8.2.1

The owner's personal insurance needs

🛡️Personal essentials — kept separate from the business

  • Disability insurance
  • Health & health savings accounts (HSAs)
  • Life insurance
  • Personal liability (consider an umbrella policy)
  • Maximize tax advantages
  • Key-person insurance for business continuity
Exam trapOwners often struggle to fund personal coverage due to cash-flow limits, time constraints, and over-emphasis on business insurance. Buy distinct business and personal policies — using business funds for personal insurance creates legal and tax problems, and a non-medical HSA withdrawal triggers a 20% penalty tax.

Learning Objective 8.2.2

Competing goals, buy-sell & continuation

⚖️Three competing goals for life insurance

Business continuity
Without a life-insurance-funded buy-sell, an owner's death can spark disputes among partners, heirs, and creditors — jeopardizing the business.
Family financial security
Without coverage the family faces immediate expenses, debts, and taxes — heirs may be forced to sell business assets.
Estate tax liabilities
Insufficient insurance leaves the estate exposed to large estate taxes — possibly forcing a sale of the whole business to pay the IRS.
SolutionsA buy-sell agreement, life insurance for family protection, and permanent life insurance for estate tax. Consider a laddered approach — multiple term policies of different durations and amounts to match evolving needs.

🤝Buy-sell agreement structures & business continuation

A buy-sell agreement is the core business-continuation tool: it funds an orderly transfer of a deceased (or departing) owner's interest, so surviving owners keep control instead of ending up with heirs or creditors as partners.

Three ways to structure a buy-sell
StructureWho buys / owns the policiesPolicies needed
Cross-purchaseEach owner buys a policy on each other owner; survivors purchase the deceased's interest directlyn × (n − 1)
Entity (stock redemption)The business entity owns a policy on each owner and redeems the deceased's interestn (one per owner)
Wait-and-seeHybrid — defers the choice of who buys (owners or entity) until the triggering event, adding flexibilityvaries

Learning Objective 8.2.3

Private retirement plan funded by life insurance

🏖️The LIRP — advantages vs. disadvantages

A life insurance retirement plan (LIRP) uses a permanent policy's cash value as a supplemental retirement vehicle.

Life insurance retirement plan trade-offs
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Tax-deferred growthHigher costs
Access to cash value (withdrawals / tax-free loans)Slower cash-value growth
Guaranteed death benefitLoans reduce the death benefit
No contribution limitsSurrender charges
Protection from market volatilityTaxes due on withdrawals above basis
Ease of managementLimited investment options
Exam trapCash value grows tax-deferred — the owner does not pay annual tax on it. The owner can access cash value in retirement, but cannot withdraw the entire death benefit tax-free — the death benefit is for the beneficiaries. A perk: emergency withdrawals before 59½ can avoid the tax penalty.

Learning Objective 8.3.1

Policies for remote employers — cyber vs fraud

💻Cyber vs. fraud (fidelity) — a key tested distinction

What each policy covers
Cyber insuranceFraud / crime (fidelity) coverage
Phishing and spoofingEmployee theft
Data-breach costsEmbezzlement
Ransomware attacksThird-party (external) fraud
Business interruptionFake invoices / vendor impersonation
Legal defense & restoration costsDishonesty & misrepresentation causing monetary loss
Exam trap — the key pointThese are DISTINCT. Cyber does not cover employee acts of dishonesty; fraud does not cover a data breach. In a two-incident question, cyber pays the breach/interruption and fraud pays the embezzlement — neither fully indemnifies both.

🏠Remote workers' compensation

Remote-employer workers' comp covers on-the-job harm for a distributed workforce:

  • Medical expenses
  • Lost wages
  • Disability benefits
  • Legal costs
Remote essentialsThe two essential policies for remote employers are cyber insurance and remote workers' compensation.

Learning Objective 8.3.2

Property & liability solutions — BOP vs CPP

📦Two primary packages

Business Owner's Policy (BOP)
Combines property and liability for smaller businesses. Each insurer has its own BOP — a package of coverages, with add-ons to customize for the business.
Commercial Package Policy (CPP)
For larger businesses. A package of monoline forms — business & contents plus general liability — that is less expensive than buying the individual policies separately.
Exam trapA CPP's premium is lower than the combined premiums of several separate monoline policies each covering one risk. Both packages are built mostly from monoline coverage.

🧱The CPP monoline categories

A CPP assembles individual "monoline" forms. The business-insurance package categories are:

Business insurance package (monoline) categories
Property-side formsLiability / other forms
Commercial propertyCommercial liability
Boiler & machineryCommercial auto
TransportationWorkers' comp & employer's liability
Crime

⚖️Commercial liability insurance — the trio

Employer's liability
Protection from employee lawsuits.
Directors' & officers' (D&O) E&O
Protection from lawsuits against the board of directors and officers.
Employment practices (EPLI)
Lawsuits due to inappropriate employment practices.
Also for businessesProfessional liability & malpractice, errors & omissions (E&O), commercial umbrella liability, and social insurance — workers' compensation and unemployment compensation.

🩺E&O vs. malpractice — who buys which

Errors & omissions (E&O) insurance is generally purchased by professionals positioned to cause their clients financial harm — financial planners, accountants, and attorneys.

Exam trapPhysicians generally purchase malpractice insurance rather than E&O. (Malpractice addresses bodily harm to patients; E&O addresses financial harm to clients.)

Kaplan questions from this module

Needs by size
How do the insurance needs of a business differ based on its size?
A. Small = basic liability; medium & large = comprehensive packages incl. property, workers' comp, cyber · B. All businesses only need liability · C. Medium-sized need the least · D. Large businesses can ignore insurance
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A

As businesses grow, risk exposure rises and coverage must become more comprehensive. Even small businesses need more than liability (property, employee injury); self-insuring is costly and unpredictable for large firms.

Risk assessment
What steps ensure a thorough risk assessment and proper coverage?
A. Focus only on financial risks · B. Identify all risks, prioritize by likelihood/impact, and review current policies for adequacy · C. Assess once every five years · D. Hand it entirely to an agent
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B

Identify various risk types, evaluate impact, and confirm policies cover them. Financial-only focus ignores employee safety; five-year cycles are too infrequent; and the owner must stay actively involved alongside the agent.

Company / agent / policy
Sarah owns Flour Power Bakery; John works for Sure Thing Insurance. To pick the optimal company, agent, and policy, Sarah should:
A. Choose the cheapest premium · B. Take the first agent to save time · C. Assess her bakery's unique risks, then compare agents by industry expertise · D. Only consider big national insurers
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C

A thorough risk assessment plus evaluation of agents/companies by industry expertise finds the best fit. Price or convenience alone can leave her underinsured; local agents may offer more personalized coverage.

Funding
Mike (Muffin Man bakery) wants property, liability, and employee-related coverage without straining cash flow. Best funding strategy?
A. Allocate 100% of profits to insurance · B. Hire an accountant to manage budget/cash flow and use tax credits/deductions · C. Insure only high-risk areas · D. Take a home-equity lump sum
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B

An accountant can offer tax advice, allocate finances efficiently, and set the optimal premium spend. 100% of profits is unsustainable; insuring only high-risk areas leaves gaps; personal savings/credit commingles personal and business finances.

Personal + business
Marcus (Couch Potato Creations LLC) — how should he protect both personal and business assets?
A. Rely solely on homeowner's insurance · B. Use an HSA for all risks · C. Use business funds for personal insurance · D. Buy distinct business and personal policies — business liability, life, disability, and an umbrella
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D

Marcus needs separate personal and business coverage. Business funds for personal insurance creates legal/tax issues; an HSA isn't comprehensive (non-medical use = 20% penalty); disability alone doesn't cover business liability, property, or health.

Competing goals
Nico & Zara own Yeast of Eden Microbrewery. Nico wants a buy-sell; Zara wants family security. Best resolution?
A. One first-to-die policy covering both equally · B. Individual policies — Nico's business-focused for the buy-sell, Zara's separate for her family · C. Nico compromises for Zara · D. Skip life insurance; save in a joint account
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B

Separate policies serve each distinct goal — the buy-sell can be a business expense; Zara funds her personal policy from her earnings. One policy rarely meets both goals, and a joint account lacks life insurance's guaranteed death protection.

LIRP
Felix (Net Results, sole proprietor) funds a private retirement plan with permanent life insurance. How does it compare to conventional plans?
A. Requires annual tax on cash value · B. Lets him withdraw the entire death benefit tax-free · C. No tax benefits · D. Cash value grows tax-deferred, accessible in retirement, plus a death benefit for heirs
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D

Cash value accumulates tax-deferred and can be accessed via withdrawals or tax-free loans, with a death benefit for heirs and no contribution limits. He cannot take the whole death benefit tax-free — it's for beneficiaries — and the cost of insurance must be weighed.

Cyber vs fraud
Zoom & Gloom Entertainment: a hacker breaches the system (shutdown + refunds), then remote worker Benny embezzles via fake invoices. How are the claims handled?
A. Cyber covers both · B. Fraud covers the breach, cyber the embezzlement · C. Cyber covers the breach (recovery + interruption); fraud covers the embezzled funds · D. Both pay equally for both
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C

Cyber covers digital breaches — data theft, recovery, business interruption. Fraud covers dishonest acts like embezzlement. Cyber doesn't cover employee dishonesty and fraud doesn't cover a data breach, so neither indemnifies both incidents.

CPP
Choose the CORRECT statement regarding a commercial package policy (CPP).
A. Provides liability insurance only · B. Does not provide comprehensive coverage · C. A BOP is often part of a CPP · D. Features lower premiums than the combined premiums of several single-risk policies
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D

A CPP bundles monoline forms at a premium lower than buying each separately. It provides comprehensive (not liability-only) coverage, and the BOP — aimed at smaller businesses — is a distinct package, not part of a CPP.

Professional liability
All of these professionals are likely to purchase errors & omissions insurance except:
A. Financial planners · B. Accountants · C. Physicians · D. Attorneys
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — physicians

Physicians generally buy malpractice insurance rather than E&O. E&O is for professionals positioned to cause clients financial harm — financial planners, accountants, and attorneys.
