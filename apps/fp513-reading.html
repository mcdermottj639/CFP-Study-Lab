LO 1.1.1 · How securities are created: investment bankers and dealers

−

Most people buy deposits and investments through an intermediary. Banks and credit unions take deposits (CDs, savings, checking) and lend to businesses, car buyers and homeowners. Brokers, dealers, investment bankers and mutual funds move money from investors into stocks, bonds and commodities. Insurers invest premiums in bonds, stocks and real estate.

An investment bank helps companies and municipalities raise capital by underwriting new securities or acting as the issuer's agent. In an IPO it leads the team (lawyers, accountants, appraisers) and usually prices the deal the day before the shares are sold to the public.

  • Investment bank functions — advise on raising long-term capital; distribute new securities; buy the issue and resell it; place large blocks; keep the issuer compliant with securities law
  • Firm commitment — underwriter buys the whole issue; unsold shares are its loss
  • Best efforts — underwriter sells what it can; issuer gets nothing for unsold shares
  • Red herring — preliminary prospectus: filed with the SEC, not yet effective, so no sales yet
  • Prospectus / registration — the offering document / filing it with the SEC
  • Green shoe — right to enlarge the offering
  • Managing (lead) underwriter → syndicate → selling group — lead bank; partner banks that help distribute; brokerage firms that help sell but aren't syndicate members
  • Seasoned (secondary) offering — an already-public company sells more stock; the book also uses "secondary offering" for sales by insiders or affiliated persons
  • Dilution — more shares outstanding lowers each share's value and EPS, so firms issue new stock mainly to rebalance debt vs. equity (e.g., debt covenants)
  • Lockup period — often 180 days after the IPO when early investors and employees can't sell, to delay selling pressure

How to tell: ask "who eats the unsold shares?" Underwriter → firm commitment.
⚖️ Commonly confused — broker vs. dealer

How to tell: agent + commission = broker; trades from its own inventory = dealer. Firms doing both are broker-dealers.
🧮 Worked example — unsold IPO shares

Fictional Kestrel Labs offers 3,000,000 shares at $12. Investors buy only 2,600,000.

  1. Firm commitment: the underwriter already bought all 3,000,000, so Kestrel receives proceeds on 3,000,000 × $12 = $36,000,000 (before fees). The underwriter is stuck with 400,000 shares.
  2. Best efforts: Kestrel receives proceeds only on 2,600,000 × $12 = $31,200,000 (before fees); the 400,000 shortfall is its problem.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: assuming best efforts still pays the issuer for every share offered.
Try it: A dealer quotes $41.20 bid / $41.35 ask. You sell 100 shares to it — what do you receive? (Answer: $4,120; the dealer buys at the bid.)
Exam trap: "Specialist" and "market maker" are both dealers — specialist on an exchange, market maker over the counter. A broker is never the principal.
Client conversation: "When you got shares before your company went public, you agreed to a lockup. Until it ends you can't sell, so let's plan the diversification for the day it lifts rather than counting on today's price."
🔬 Go deeper — why underwriters price the day before+

Under a firm commitment the underwriter buys at a pre-set price and resells to the public, hoping for a spread. Pricing as late as possible (the day before the sale) limits its exposure to market moves. A dealer's bid and ask move toward the price that balances supply and demand.

LO 1.1.1 · Venture capital, private placements and limited partnerships

−

Venture capital funds private (often start-up) companies, usually through convertible preferred stock. It is high risk, high potential return, illiquid, and has low correlation with stocks. VC firms take a big equity stake, advise management, and hope to exit in about 5–10 years, ideally through an IPO; they expect a few big winners to offset outright losses.

VC stageWhat the money is for
SeedProduct development and market research before any product exists
Start-upInitial marketing (not sales)
First stageManufacturing and sales
Second stageWorking capital
MezzanineExpansion and new products
BridgeGetting to an expected IPO
AcquisitionBuying other companies (may include high-yield bonds)
LBOLetting management buy all or part of a business, often a division a public company is shedding

Private placements sell an issue (most often bonds) to a small group of institutions or sophisticated investors. They skip SEC registration and IPO selling costs, keep company information private, and can be custom-tailored. Limit: 35 non-accredited buyers, unlimited accredited buyers (2026 edition figure). Insurers and pension funds are frequent buyers.

  • Accredited investor (individual) — net worth over $1 million alone or with spouse, excluding the primary residence; or income over $200,000 ($300,000 joint) in each of the last two years with the same expected this year (2026 edition figure)
  • Accredited (entities and insiders) — banks, insurers, registered investment companies, BDCs, SBICs; ERISA plans with a bank/insurer/RIA making decisions or assets over $5 million; charities, corporations or partnerships with assets over $5 million; the issuer's directors, executive officers or general partners; businesses owned entirely by accredited investors; trusts over $5 million directed by a sophisticated person
⚖️ Commonly confused — general partner vs. limited partner
General partnerLimited partner
ManagementRuns the business; decides distributionsNo role
LiabilityUnlimitedLimited
How to tell: control and unlimited liability travel together.

LP pros: share in a venture with limited liability, split start-up costs, periodic income. Cons: riskier than bonds or listed stocks, usually illiquid, no management voice, restricted resale. Income is passed through (not tax-free).

🧮 Worked example — is the client accredited?

Fictional Nadia, single: net worth $1.3 million, including $450,000 of equity in her home; income $210,000 last year and $190,000 the year before.

  1. Net worth test: $1,300,000 − $450,000 = $850,000 → fails (needs over $1 million).
  2. Income test: needs over $200,000 in each of the two most recent years → $190,000 fails.
  3. Result: not accredited.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: counting home equity; averaging two years of income.
Try it: A married couple earned $310,000 jointly each of the last two years and expects the same. Accredited? (Answer: yes — joint income over $300,000.)
Exam trap: Venture capital is illiquid. Any answer calling it highly liquid is wrong.
Client conversation: "This partnership may sound like a tax shelter, but you'd have no say in how it's run, you probably couldn't sell it when you need cash, and the income isn't tax-free. A diversified fund gives you professional management without locking you in."

LO 1.2.1 · Primary and secondary markets, orders and custody

−

The primary market is the first sale of new securities; the cash (less underwriting costs) goes to the issuer. The secondary market is where investors trade existing securities with each other. Fund-only investors may never touch either directly; their fund managers do.

Secondary marketWhat trades
FirstExchange (auction) trading of listed securities, e.g., NYSE
SecondUnlisted securities, OTC and Nasdaq
ThirdExchange-listed securities traded OTC, mainly by institutions cutting costs
FourthInstitutions trading big blocks directly with each other, no broker (e.g., INSTINET)
  • NYSE listing minimums — at least 400 round-lot holders and 1.1 million public shares, plus earnings or valuation/revenue tests
  • Regional exchanges / dual listing — smaller local companies that may also trade on another exchange; non-listed firms can use Nasdaq
  • Public float — shares actually available to trade; the rest are mostly insider-held with sale restrictions
  • Round lot / odd lot — usually 100 shares / fewer than 100
  • Street name vs. certificate — held by the broker for you vs. a certificate issued to you; street-name risk is broker failure
  • SIPC — liquidates failed brokerages; covers up to $500,000 per customer, max $250,000 cash (2026 edition figure); no cover for market losses
OrderWhat it does
MarketFills now at the best available price; no price control
LimitCeiling to buy / floor to sell; "or better" is implied
DayExpires at the close if unfilled
GTCLives until filled or cancelled (brokers may cap it, e.g., 90 days)
StopTrades at/through the stop → becomes a market order. Sell stop below market; buy stop above
Stop-limitTrades at/through the stop → becomes a limit order
🧮 Worked example — stop vs. stop-limit on a gap down

Fictional Ana owns 500 shares bought at $58. She enters a GTC sell order at $50, either as a stop or as a stop-limit. The stock closes at $51, bad news hits overnight, and it opens at $43.

  1. Stop at $50: the open at $43 is "through" the stop, so it becomes a market order and fills near $43.
  2. Stop-limit at $50: it becomes a limit order to sell at $50 or better — nothing fills at $43. If the stock keeps sliding, Ana still owns it.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: thinking a stop guarantees the stop price; placing a buy stop below the market.
Try it: A client short at $28 wants protection at $33. Which order? (Answer: buy stop at $33, or buy stop-limit at $33 to cap the fill price.)
Exam trap: Only the market order places no restriction on price. Stop = execution certain, price not; stop-limit = price certain, execution not.
Client conversation: "A stop gets you out, but on a bad-news morning it may be well below your number. A stop-limit protects the price but may leave you holding the stock. Which risk bothers you more?"

LO 1.2.1 · Short sales, margin and leveraged returns

−

Short selling profits when a price falls: borrow shares through a broker (posting a deposit = margin requirement, currently 50%, times market value, in a margin account), sell them, later buy them back, and return (cover) them. Short sellers are bearish or hedging; any dividend paid while short must be paid to the lender. There's no deadline to cover. Each lendable share in margin accounts can back only one short sale.

Margin is leverage: controlling more property than the cash invested.

  • Initial margin — 50% under Fed Regulation T (2026 edition figure); brokers may require more
  • Maintenance margin — set by the broker, usually 35%; NYSE Rule 431(b) floor of 25%; always below initial
  • Debit balance — amount owed to the broker: the loan plus accrued interest
  • Equity — current market value − debit balance
  • Margin call — broker's demand for cash when equity falls below maintenance; if unmet, the broker sells part of the position
  • Call loan (call money) rate — rate a bank charges a broker that borrows using customers' margin securities as collateral
Short sale return: (sale proceeds − cost to cover − dividends paid) ÷ (proceeds × initial margin %)
Margin-call price: [purchase price × (1 − initial margin %)] ÷ (1 − maintenance margin %)
= loan per share ÷ (1 − maintenance %)
Deposit needed: (current price × maintenance %) − (current price − loan per share), times shares
HPR on margin: (ending value − beginning value ± cash flows) ÷ investor's initial investment
Dividends are + cash flows; margin interest is a − cash flow.
🧮 Worked example — short sale with a dividend

Fictional Wes shorts 100 shares at $80 (50% margin), pays a $0.50 dividend, and covers at $70.

  1. Deposit: 100 × $80 × 0.50 = $4,000
  2. Gross gain: $8,000 − $7,000 = $1,000; less dividend $50 → $950
  3. Return: $950 ÷ $4,000 = 23.75%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: adding the dividend; dividing by full proceeds instead of the deposit.
🧮 Worked example — margin call and deposit

Fictional Lena buys 1,000 shares at $40 with 50% initial margin; maintenance is 35%. Loan = $20 a share.

  1. Margin-call price: $20 ÷ (1 − 0.35) = $20 ÷ 0.65 = $30.77
  2. Stock falls to $26. Required equity: $26 × 0.35 = $9.10. Actual equity: $26 − $20 = $6.00.
  3. Deposit: ($9.10 − $6.00) × 1,000 = $3,100
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using the original purchase price for required equity; dividing by the initial margin instead of (1 − maintenance).
🧮 Worked example — HPR with and without margin

Fictional Omar buys 400 shares at $25 (50% margin, 6% interest), no dividends, sells at $30 after one year.

  1. Gain: $12,000 − $10,000 = $2,000; interest 6% × $5,000 = $300
  2. Margin HPR: ($2,000 − $300) ÷ $5,000 = 34%
  3. Cash HPR: $2,000 ÷ $10,000 = 20%
Try it: Same trade but the stock falls to $22. Margin HPR? (Answer: (−$1,200 − $300) ÷ $5,000 = −30%, vs. −12% unleveraged.)
Exam trap: For the deposit, use the current price, not the purchase price. For margin HPR, divide by the investor's own money, not the full position.
Client conversation: "Borrowing to invest doubles the swing both ways. If the stock drops about a quarter, your broker can demand cash within days or sell for you at the bottom. Let's size this so a margin call can't force your hand."
🔬 Go deeper — why short shares must be located+

Each borrowed share is earmarked to one short sale so total shorts can't exceed the lendable shares in margin accounts. Otherwise more shares could be shorted than exist, artificially pushing the price down.

LO 1.3.1 · Cash and cash equivalents

−

In planning, "cash" means bank deposits and money market instruments with high liquidity. These are money market (short-term) rather than capital market assets. Interest is ordinary income; a sale can technically produce a capital gain or loss, but because these are held short term for income, that rarely happens.

🧮 Worked example — T-bill return and after-tax edge

Fictional June buys a 13-week T-bill, $10,000 face, for $9,900 and holds to maturity. She's in a 22% federal and 5% state bracket (ignore interaction).

  1. Income = $10,000 − $9,900 = $100; HPR = $100 ÷ $9,900 = 1.01%
  2. Tax: federal only → $100 × 22% = $22
  3. Same $100 from a CD: $100 × 27% = $27 → the T-bill saves $5 of state tax
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing by face value; taxing T-bill income at the state level.
Try it: A dealer sells Treasuries for $2,994,000 and repurchases in 3 days for $3,000,000. Interest and repo rate? (Answer: $6,000; $6,000 ÷ $2,994,000 ≈ 0.20% for 3 days.)
Exam trap: MMDA = bank product, FDIC-insured. Money market mutual fund = investment company, not insured. Commercial paper max maturity is 270 days; Eurodollars run under 6 months.
Client conversation: "Cash is great for your emergency fund and near-term bills. Beyond that, inflation quietly eats it. For a retiree, losing buying power is often a bigger risk than losing principal."
🔬 Go deeper — relative traits of money market instruments+

The textbook rates T-bills, banker's acceptances and Euro CDs high on default safety; negotiable CDs and commercial paper moderately high; repos moderate. Repos are the only one rated low on marketability. All carry low market risk, with Euro CDs "moderately low."

LO 1.4.1 · Taxing interest, dividends and net investment income

−

Investors are generally taxed on interest, dividends and capital gains in the year received. Securities inside qualified plans such as 401(k)s aren't currently taxed.

IncomeFederalState
U.S. Treasury interestYesNo
Corporate bond interestYesYes
Municipal bond interestNo (limited exceptions)Yes, unless issued in home state
DividendsYesYes
Capital gainsYesYes
  • Qualified dividends — from C corporations; taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% (2026 edition figure), set by taxable income including the dividends. The book cites the MFJ 0% band ending around $94,050 (2026 edition figure; check the current Annual Limits)
  • Holding period — more than 60 days in the 121-day window starting 60 days before the ex-dividend date (2026 edition figure)
  • Non-qualified — REIT and S corporation dividends (no entity-level tax first)
  • Return of capital — not taxed; lowers basis
  • Stock dividends — generally not federally taxable
  • Corporate shareholders — a dividend exclusion lowers their effective rate
  • NIIT — 3.8% × lesser of net investment income or MAGI over threshold ($250,000 MFJ, $125,000 MFS, $200,000 others; 2026 edition figure)
NIIT: 3.8% × lesser of (net investment income, MAGI − threshold)
Net investment income = investment income − certain deductible investment expenses (e.g., investment interest expense, penalty for early withdrawal of savings)
🧮 Worked example — NIIT, two couples

Fictional joint filers, each with $60,000 of net investment income.

  1. The Parks: MAGI $410,000 → excess $160,000; lesser = $60,000 → 3.8% × $60,000 = $2,280
  2. The Cruzes: MAGI $290,000 → excess $40,000; lesser = $40,000 → 3.8% × $40,000 = $1,520
⚠️ Common wrong methods: taxing all NII regardless of MAGI; applying 3.8% to the full MAGI; using the wrong filing-status threshold.
Try it: Single filer, MAGI $215,000, NII $40,000. NIIT? (Answer: 3.8% × $15,000 = $570.)
Exam trap: "Dividend" doesn't always mean preferential rates — REIT and S corp payouts don't qualify, and neither does a C corp dividend if the holding period is missed.
Client conversation: "Your Treasury interest skips state tax, your in-state munis skip both, and your REIT dividends are taxed like wages. Where each holding sits can change your tax bill without changing your risk."

LO 1.4.1 · Capital gain netting and wash sales

−

Held more than one year = long term; one year or less = short term (2026 edition figure). Net long-term gains get the same 0%/15%/20% rates as qualified dividends. Exceptions: collectibles (art, metals, gems, antiques, coins, stamps, and generally gold/silver ETFs) max 28%; gain from real-estate depreciation max 25% (2026 edition figure).

StepAction
1Net ST gains vs. ST losses
2Net LT gains vs. LT losses
3If one side is a gain and the other a loss, net to one figure
4Net ST gain → ordinary rates; net LT gain → 0/15/20%; net loss → deduct up to $3,000 a year (2026 edition figure), carry the rest forward indefinitely

Basis: stocks and bonds (corporate, Treasury, municipal, OID) generally start at cost; for mutual funds, basis = cost + reinvested income.

Wash sale: sell stock or securities at a loss and acquire similar securities within 30 days before or after (2026 edition figure). The loss is postponed, not lost: it is added to the replacement shares' basis. For bonds, the book says buying a similar bond (coupon, term, quality) from a different issuer avoids the rule.

Wash-sale basis: new basis = cost of replacement shares + disallowed loss
🧮 Worked example — netting

Fictional Grant: LT gain $14,000, LT loss $5,000, ST gain $3,000, ST loss $18,000.

  1. LT: $14,000 − $5,000 = +$9,000
  2. ST: $3,000 − $18,000 = −$15,000
  3. Cross-net: $9,000 − $15,000 = −$6,000 net short-term loss
  4. Deduct $3,000 this year; carry $3,000 forward
⚠️ Common wrong methods: skipping the separate ST and LT steps; deducting the whole loss; treating the excess as forfeited.
🧮 Worked example — wash-sale basis

Fictional Sofia bought 150 shares for $9,600 ($64 each). She sells all at $52 ($7,800), then buys 150 shares back 10 days later at $50 ($7,500).

  1. Realized loss: $9,600 − $7,800 = $1,800
  2. Recognized loss: $0 (repurchase within 30 days)
  3. New basis: $7,500 + $1,800 = $9,300 ($62 a share)
⚠️ Common wrong methods: subtracting the loss from the new cost; thinking only purchases after the sale count; thinking a year-end crossover avoids the rule.
Try it: Sold at $20 (cost $31), repurchased 12 days later at $22. New per-share basis? (Answer: $22 + $11 = $33.)
Exam trap: The $3,000 limit applies to the net loss after cross-netting. And the wash-sale window looks 30 days backward as well as forward.
Client conversation: "We can book this loss to offset gains, but if we buy the same stock back within a month the IRS just tacks the loss onto the new shares. Let's either wait out the window or hold something similar but not the same in the meantime."

LO 2.1.1 · Bond vocabulary, maturity and ratings

−

A bond is a loan to a government, municipality or company: the issuer pays coupons and returns principal at maturity. The contract behind it is the indenture.

  • Indenture / covenants — the issuer–bondholder contract; covenants are its promises and prohibitions. A sinking fund sets money aside yearly to retire the debt, lowering default risk.
  • Registered / bearer / book-entry — pay the owner of record / pay whoever holds it / ownership kept electronically at a central depository.
  • Par (face) value — principal repaid at maturity; exam default $1,000
  • Coupon rate (nominal yield) — annual interest as a % of par; exam default: paid semiannually
  • Basis point — 1/100 of 1% (a 0.75% move = 75 bp).
  • Discount / premium — trading below / above par. Issued below par = original issue discount (OID).
  • Call provision — lets the issuer retire the bond early, usually above par, typically after rates fall.
  • Secured bond vs debenture — specific assets pledged vs unsecured (higher yield for the same issuer and term).
  • Term vs serial bond — one maturity date vs a series of maturities (municipalities are the biggest serial issuers).

🧮 Worked example — coupon and basis points

A $1,000 par bond has a 3.5% coupon. Its yield moves from 4.20% to 4.95%.

  2. Yield change = 0.75 percentage points = 75 basis points.
  3. Yield above coupon → the bond trades at a discount.

LO 2.3.1 · Immunization, ladders, barbells and bullets

−

Immunization sets interest rate (price) risk against reinvestment risk. Rates up: prices fall, but coupons reinvest higher. Rates down: the reverse. Full offset when portfolio duration = liability duration (time horizon). Used mainly by pensions, endowments and insurers; individuals use it for goals like tuition.

  • Zero-coupon bond — duration = maturity, so one zero maturing on the goal date immunizes it.
  • Coupon bond — duration < maturity, so pick a maturity somewhat beyond the horizon.
  • Rebalancing — portfolio duration falls faster than liability duration; immunization is not fully passive.
StructureShapeWhy use itManagement
LadderEqual amounts at staggered maturitiesMore yield than all-short; steady cash to roll to the long endPassive, long term
Barbell (dumbbell)Short + long onlyLong end locks rates; short end frees cash if bonds fallActive
BulletBought over time, all mature on one dateMatch duration to one cash need; limit rate riskPlanned purchases

In both ladders and barbells, short bonds limit losses if rates rise; long bonds offer big gains if rates fall.

🧮 Worked example — building a ladder and a bullet

Ladder: $150,000 split into $30,000 at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years. Bullet: $90,000 needed in 8 years.

  1. Ladder: each year the maturing $30,000 rung buys a new 5-year bond, keeping the 1–5 year shape.
  2. Bullet: buy an 8-year bond now, a 6-year bond in year 2, a 4-year bond in year 4 — all mature in year 8.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: calling short + long only a ladder (it's a barbell); matching maturity rather than duration to the horizon with coupon bonds.
Try it: Horizon 7 years. Which immunizes: a 7-year zero or a 7-year 6% coupon bond? (The zero)
Exam trap: a ladder yields more than an all-short portfolio, and a barbell (not a ladder) is the active one.
Client conversation: “We'll stagger your bonds so one matures every year. If rates rise, the money coming due gets reinvested at the higher rate; if they fall, the longer bonds have already locked in today's rate.”

−

A hybrid: a fixed dividend set as a % of par, but the board must declare it. Paid before common dividends. No maturity (a perpetuity), so price moves inversely with rates — more than the same firm's bonds.

  • Buyers — investors wanting a higher yield than the common and a steady cash flow.
  • Dividends-received deduction — corporate holders deduct 50% (own <20%) or 65% (own 20%+), within limits (2026 edition figure).
  • Convertible preferred — higher dividend than common, upside via conversion, priority over common; popular for funding venture capital and private equity.
Like bonds
  • Fixed periodic cash flow

  • Callable/redeemable; may be convertible
  • May have sinking funds
  • Ahead of common in bankruptcy
Unlike bonds
  • Dividends not deductible by issuer
  • DRD applies to dividends, not interest
  • Missed dividend ≠ bankruptcy

LO 3.2.1 · Cash dividends, dates, DRIPs and taxation

−

A dividend exists only once the board declares it, unlike bond interest, which is owed. Common holders are paid after preferred holders. Only shareholders of record on the record date receive it.

  • Payout ratio — dividends per share ÷ EPS.
  • DRIP — dividends buy more shares straight from the issuer, little or no commission, sometimes at a discount; extra cash often allowed.
Dividend payout ratio = dividend per share ÷ earnings per share

LO 4.1.1 · Bond prices and interest rates

−

A bond is worth the present value of the cash it will pay you: every coupon plus par at maturity, discounted at today's market yield for bonds of similar quality and term. Because the coupon is fixed when the bond is issued, the price is the only thing that can move when market rates change — so price and yield always move in opposite directions.

  • Intrinsic value (bond) — PV of future coupons and par at the market yield (YTM)
  • Discount bond — market rate above coupon → price below par
  • Premium bond — market rate below coupon → price above par
  • Current yield (CY) — annual coupon ÷ price; ignores the move back to par
  • YTM / YTC — yields that include the price drifting to par at maturity (YTM) or to the call price at the call date (YTC)
Bond value: PV = CF1 ÷ (1+y)1 + CF2 ÷ (1+y)2 + … + CFt ÷ (1+y)t
where CF = semiannual coupons and par, y = market yield per period, t = number of cash flows.
Calculator (2 P/YR): N = years × 2 · I/YR = annual market yield · PMT = annual coupon ÷ 2 · FV = 1,000 → solve PV.
🧮 Worked example — pricing a discount bond

Corinne is weighing a 15-year, $1,000 bond with a 4% coupon paid semiannually. Comparable bonds yield 5.5%.

  1. Predict first: market 5.5% > coupon 4% → expect a discount.
  2. N = 15 × 2 = 30; I/YR = 5.5; PMT = 40 ÷ 2 = 20; FV = 1,000.
  3. Solve PV = $848.13.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: annual compounding (N = 15, PMT = 40) gives $849.44; typing 2.75 into I/YR with 2 P/YR already set halves the rate twice and gives a premium of $1,152.79; using the coupon as the discount rate always returns $1,000.
Try it: 8-year bond, 6.5% semiannual coupon, comparable yield 5%. Answer: N 16, I/YR 5, PMT 32.50, FV 1,000 → $1,097.91 (premium).
⚖️ Commonly confused — yield rankings at a premium vs. a discount
Bond trades atHighestMiddleLowest
PremiumCYYTMYTC
DiscountYTCYTMCY
Par (no call premium)CY = YTM = YTC
How to tell: YTM and YTC count the trip back to par. A premium shrinks on that trip (bad, and faster if called); a discount grows on it (good, and faster if called). CY ignores the trip entirely.
Exam trap: for a bond you already own, price it on its remaining term at the yield for that term — not the original maturity or the original yield.
Client conversation: “Your bond's coupon never changes. If new bonds start paying more, nobody will pay full price for yours — its price drops until a buyer earns the same as a new bond. If rates fall, the reverse happens and your bond is worth more than you paid.”
🔬 Go deeper — why a call is the worst case for a premium bond+

With a premium bond you paid more than you will get back. Spreading that loss over the full term (YTM) hurts less than absorbing it by an earlier call date (YTC), so YTC is the lowest yield and the one to plan around. For a discount bond the logic flips: an early call hands you the gain back to par sooner, so YTC is highest.

−

Duration is the weighted-average time, in years, until you collect a bond's interest and principal measured in present-value dollars. Picture a seesaw holding each discounted cash flow: the balance point is the duration. Because it sums up timing and size of cash flows, it doubles as the standard gauge of interest-rate risk — the bond-market cousin of beta.

LO 4.1.2 · Estimating price change with duration

−

The quick estimate is duration × rate change. The textbook's “more precise” version divides the rate change by (1 + current YTM). The minus sign builds in the inverse relationship.

Rule of thumb: %ΔP ≈ −D × Δy
Textbook formula: ΔP ÷ P = −D × [Δy ÷ (1 + y)]
D = (Macaulay) duration · Δy = rate change as a decimal (50 bp = 0.0050) · y = current YTM, not the coupon.
Semiannual convention: use y ÷ 2 in the denominator; keep D and Δy unchanged.
Dollar change = %ΔP × current price.
🧮 Worked example — rates rise 75 bp

Nadia's bond: price $980, duration 6.2 (annual), YTM 5%.

  1. Δy ÷ (1 + y) = 0.0075 ÷ 1.05 = 0.00714
  2. −6.2 × 0.00714 = −0.0443 → about −4.43%
  3. −0.0443 × $980 = −$43.40 → new price ≈ $936.60
  4. Semiannual version of a different bond (D 5.0, YTM 7%, $1,000, +1%): −5.0 × 0.01 ÷ 1.035 = −4.83% → $951.69 (annual would give $953.27).
⚠️ Common wrong methods: plugging in the coupon rate for y; forgetting the (1 + y) divisor ($934.43 here); adding instead of subtracting when rates rise; halving Δy as well as y in the semiannual version.
Try it: price $1,050, duration 8.4, YTM 6%, rates fall 50 bp. Answer: −8.4 × (−0.005 ÷ 1.06) = +3.96% → +$41.60 → about $1,091.60.

LO 4.1.2 · Convexity

−

Duration draws a straight line through a curved price–yield relationship. For small rate moves (generally under 1%) the line is close enough; for bigger moves the curve matters. Convexity measures the gap between the curve and the line. Total price change = change from duration + change from convexity.

  • Positive convexity — typical of straight (noncallable) bonds; gains beat the duration estimate when rates fall, losses are smaller when rates rise
  • Negative convexity — callable and mortgage-backed bonds; gains lag and losses exceed the duration estimate
  • Most convexity — low coupon, low YTM, long maturity (same pattern as duration)
🧮 Worked example — reading the curve

A noncallable bond has duration 6. Rates move 2.5 points.

  1. Duration alone: 6 × 2.5 = ±15%.
  2. Rates fall: positive convexity → actual gain is more than 15%.
  3. Rates rise: actual loss is less than 15%.
  4. Swap in a callable bond with the same duration: gain less than 15%, loss more than 15%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: scaling duration linearly for big moves (2 points ≠ exactly 2 × the 1-point change); assuming convexity helps callable bonds.
Try it: rates are expected to fall sharply. Which gains less than duration predicts — a long zero or a callable corporate? Answer: the callable corporate (negative convexity).
⚖️ Commonly confused — positive vs. negative convexity
Rates fallRates riseTypical bonds
PositiveGain > duration estimateLoss < duration estimateStraight bonds
NegativeGain < duration estimateLoss > duration estimateCallable, mortgage-backed
How to tell: positive convexity is “heads I win more, tails I lose less.” Anything the issuer can call or the borrower can prepay flips that.
Exam trap: the Board does not expect a convexity calculation — only its direction. Duration understates price increases and overstates price decreases for positively convex bonds.
Client conversation: “This callable bond pays a bit more, but if rates fall the issuer can call it, so it won't rise much. If rates rise, it can fall harder than a regular bond. You're being paid extra yield for giving the issuer that option.”

LO 4.2.2 · Financial leverage, ROE and dividend growth

−

Borrowing shrinks the equity base, so the same earnings look bigger per dollar of equity: ROE and EPS rise. The price is fixed interest that lowers net income and becomes a burden in a contraction. Leverage also feeds straight into the dividend growth rate used in valuation.

ROE = net income ÷ equity (equity = assets − liabilities)
ROA = net income ÷ total assets
Sustainable growth: g = ROE × RR, where RR (retention rate) = 1 − payout ratio
🧮 Worked example — leverage and growth

Two firms raise $40M each. Firm X: all equity. Firm Y: $15M equity + $25M debt. Each earns $4M (ignore interest).

  1. ROE X = 4 ÷ 40 = 10.0%; ROE Y = 4 ÷ 15 = 26.7%.
  2. Halcyon Corp.: assets $800M, liabilities $300M → equity $500M; net income $60M → ROE 12%; ROA 7.5%.
  3. Dividends $24M → retention = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60 → g = 0.12 × 0.60 = 7.2%.
  4. If payout rose so retention fell to 0.40: g = 0.12 × 0.40 = 4.8% — more payout, slower growth.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying ROE by the payout ratio instead of retention; using ROA in place of ROE; dividing earnings by total assets or by liabilities when ROE is asked.
Try it: keep Halcyon's $800M of assets and $60M of earnings but raise liabilities to $600M. New ROE? Answer: 60 ÷ 200 = 30% — leverage alone more than doubled it.
⚖️ Commonly confused — ROE vs. ROA as a leverage signal
Low or no debtHeavy debt
ROA vs. ROEClose together (equal with zero debt)ROA a small fraction of ROE
In a contractionNo fixed interest to carryFixed interest strains earnings; debt hard to pay down
How to tell: a dazzling ROE with a modest ROA means the shareholders' return is being borrowed, not earned on the assets.
Exam trap: high retention is right when the company can reinvest at a high ROE; a high payout is justified only when it cannot. And very high g values rarely last — growth tends to slow as companies mature.
Client conversation: “This company's 40% return on equity looks great, but most of it comes from borrowing. In a recession those interest payments don't shrink. The debt-free competitor earns less on paper but is likely to hold up better when business slows.”

LO 5.1.1 · Direct real estate: NOI, cap rates, leverage, raw land

−

Real assets (real estate, gold, collectibles) can be touched and often have low or negative correlation with stocks and bonds. A home is a personal-use asset on the balance sheet; investment real estate is bought for income or appreciation. Its two big draws: leverage (mostly borrowed money) and tax deductions (mortgage interest, property taxes, depreciation).

  • PGI — potential gross income: all rents plus other income (laundry, parking)
  • EGI — effective gross income: PGI minus vacancy and collection losses
  • NOI — EGI minus cash operating expenses; never subtract depreciation or debt service
  • Cap rate — the discount rate used to turn NOI into value; estimated from comparable recent sales, then adjusted
  • Undeveloped land — passive, no income, ongoing costs; held only for large appreciation
NOI ladder: Rents + other income = PGI → − vacancy/collection = EGI → − cash operating expenses = NOI
Value: V = NOI ÷ cap rate
Leveraged return on equity: (sale price − purchase price) ÷ down payment
🧮 Worked example — Maple Court (fictional)

20 units at $1,200/month; other income $6,000/yr; vacancy 6% of PGI; cash operating expenses $98,000; mortgage interest $61,000; depreciation $25,000. Comparable cap rate 7%.

  1. Gross rents = 20 × $1,200 × 12 = $288,000; PGI = $288,000 + $6,000 = $294,000
  2. Vacancy = 6% × $294,000 = $17,640 → EGI = $276,360
  3. NOI = $276,360 − $98,000 = $178,360 (interest and depreciation ignored)
  4. Value = $178,360 ÷ 0.07 = $2,548,000 — the most a prudent buyer should pay
⚠️ Common wrong methods: subtracting mortgage interest or depreciation; skipping vacancy; multiplying NOI by the cap rate; capitalizing EGI instead of NOI.
Try it: If comparable sales point to an 8% cap rate instead, what is Maple Court worth? ($178,360 ÷ 0.08 = $2,229,500 — a higher cap rate means a lower value.)

A derivative takes its value from another asset. An option gives the buyer (holder) a right — not an obligation — to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at the strike (exercise) price on or before expiration. The writer (seller) collects the premium and takes on the obligation. One contract = 100 shares. The Options Clearing Corporation guarantees listed contracts. Most options run under a year; LEAPS run for years.

  2. $55 put priced $4.60: intrinsic = $55 − $52 = $3.00; time value = $1.60; in the money; buyer's breakeven $50.40
  3. A $55 call would be out of the money (intrinsic $0); a $50 put also out of the money
  4. Writer of the $55 put: max gain $460 per contract; max loss ($55 − $4.60) × 100 = $5,040

LO 5.2.2 · Using options: speculation, covered calls, protective puts

−

The three most common uses: buying options to speculate, writing covered calls for income, and buying protective puts to hedge.

  • Speculation — more leverage than margin (margin needs at least 50% cash under Fed rules, 2026 edition figure; an option may cost ~5% of the stock); loss capped at premium; but options expire, so the move must come in time
  • Covered call — own the shares, write calls; keep the premium, give up gains above the strike; best in a sideways market
  • Premium drivers — stock price vs. strike, time left, volatility
  • Protective put — own the shares, buy puts; floor at the strike; costly and temporary
  • Index puts — hedge a whole portfolio (“portfolio insurance”); match the index to the holdings
  • Buying time — buy a call (or put) to lock in a position while finishing research
  • Hedging a short sale — buy a call to cap the unlimited loss from a rising stock
🧮 Worked example — covered call vs. protective put

Nadia owns 200 shares bought at $30 and writes 2 calls, $33 strike, $1.20 premium ($240 total).

  1. Stock ends at $36: shares called at $33 → gain ($33 − $30 + $1.20) × 200 = $840; the move above $33 belongs to the buyer
  2. Stock ends at $31: calls expire; she keeps the $240 and the shares
  3. Stock ends at $25: loss ($25 − $30 + $1.20) × 200 = −$760; the premium cushions only $1.20/share. Breakeven = $30 − $1.20 = $28.80
  4. Protective put instead: own at $50, buy a $47 put for $1.50 → max loss = ($50 − $47) + $1.50 = $4.50/share; upside kept, less $1.50
⚠️ Common wrong methods: letting the covered call writer keep gains above the strike; forgetting the put premium when computing the floor; calling covered calls “safe” in a crash.
Try it: A speculator buys a $40 call for $1.50. Above what price does she profit at expiration? ($41.50.)

🔬 Go deeper — buying time with a call+

An investor who likes a $30 stock but needs weeks to finish research can buy a $31 call for $0.80. If the stock runs to $36, exercising gets shares at $31 (all-in $31.80) instead of chasing at $36. If it drops to $28, the call lapses and the all-in cost of buying then is $28.80 — still below buying at $30 today.

LO 5.3.1 · Futures: terms, margin, hedging and taxation

−

A futures contract obligates two parties to make or take delivery of a set amount of a commodity or financial asset (Treasuries, indices, currencies) at a future date and price. Contracts are standardized and exchange-traded (e.g., CME, NYMEX); only about 1% end in delivery — most are closed with an offsetting contract. Long = agree to buy (gains if price rises); short = agree to sell (gains if price falls). Unlike an option buyer, a futures trader can lose far more than the deposit.

  • Initial margin — good faith deposit / performance bond, roughly 5–10% of contract value; hedgers post less than speculators because they can deliver
  • Maintenance margin — if equity drops below it, deposit enough to restore the initial margin, immediately
  • Marked to market — daily gains/losses posted to account equity
  • Spot price — cash price now; futures converge to it at expiration
  • Daily price limit — max move from prior settlement
  • Open interest — contracts outstanding
  • Short hedge — own the asset, sell futures (farmer, rancher, stock or bond holder)
  • Long hedge — need the asset later, buy futures (baker, cereal maker, meat packer)
Gain/loss = (new price − old price) × contract size × number of contracts (sign flips for a short)
Index futures = index-point change × multiplier × contracts
Tax: year-end net gain/loss = 60% long-term + 40% short-term capital (2026 edition figure)
🧮 Worked example — a margin call (fictional contract)

A speculator goes long one 1,000-barrel contract at $70. Initial margin $6,000; maintenance $4,500.

  1. Price settles at $68.20: loss = $1.80 × 1,000 = $1,800
  2. Equity = $6,000 − $1,800 = $4,200 < $4,500 → margin call
  3. Deposit to restore initial margin: $6,000 − $4,200 = $1,800 (not just $300)
  4. Index example: long 1 contract (multiplier 250) from 4,500 to 4,520 → +20 × $250 = $5,000
  5. If year-end net futures gain is $9,000: $5,400 long-term, $3,600 short-term
⚠️ Common wrong methods: topping up only to maintenance; forgetting the contract count or multiplier; using actual holding period for tax.
Try it: A pension manager fears rising interest rates on a bond portfolio. Buy or sell bond futures? (Sell.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — short hedge vs. long hedge

How to tell: the hedge is always opposite to the cash position. If the business would simply wait for lower prices, no long hedge is needed.
Exam trap: futures gains are capital gains even when the underlying (corn, cattle) would be inventory, and the 60/40 split applies regardless of how long the contract was held.
Client conversation: “Futures are like a deposit on a deal that settles every single day. Small moves can wipe out the deposit and demand more money overnight, which is why most advisors use them only for hedging or through managed accounts.”
🔬 Go deeper — why hedgers lock in, not profit+

Futures markets move price risk from producers and processors to speculators. A hedger's gain or loss on the futures offsets the opposite result in the cash market, so the hedger neither profits nor loses from later price moves — the price is locked. Index futures (S&P 500, Russell 2000, Nasdaq 100, MidCap 400) and Treasury note/bond futures let advisors hedge stock and bond portfolios; multipliers vary by contract.

LO 5.4.1 · Tangible assets: collectibles, gold, natural resources

−

Tangibles are collectibles (cards, coins, stamps, art) and precious metals. Collectibles offer enjoyment plus possible appreciation but trade in inefficient, illiquid markets. Precious metals get value from forces outside financial markets (e.g., fear of inflation), so they often move opposite to stocks — a hedge for a bearish stock market.

  • Collectibles tax rate — max 28% long-term (2026 edition figure), higher than for securities
  • Tax shelter traits — no tax until sale; usually no current income; step-up in basis at death
  • Gold's enemy — high real interest rates (no income, high opportunity cost); negative real rates help
  • Direct gold — bullion or bullion coins near spot price; storage burden; return only from appreciation; check how gold ETFs are built (actual metal vs. futures)
  • Gold miners — ~2× as volatile as large caps; foreign, currency and cost risks plus fly-by-night operators — prefer funds or large established firms
  • Allocation — an advisor may cap gold at about 5–10% of a diversified portfolio
  • Natural resources — oil, gas, timber via LPs, company stocks, or sector funds; depletion and intangible drilling cost deductions pass through; high risk
🧮 Worked example — the 28% difference

Paolo realizes a $30,000 long-term gain. His long-term rate on securities is 15%.

  1. Gain from selling stock: $30,000 × 15% = $4,500
  2. Same gain from a coin collection, if taxed at the 28% cap: $30,000 × 28% = $8,400
  3. If he instead holds the coins until death, heirs get a stepped-up basis and the gain escapes capital gains tax
⚠️ Common wrong methods: applying the 0/15/20% securities rates to collectibles; assuming tangibles throw off taxable income each year.
Try it: Real rates are high and rising. Is that good or bad for gold? (Bad.)
Exam trap: a retiree heavy in tangibles faces liquidity risk — being forced to sell into a weak, illiquid market to raise income.
Client conversation: “Collect what you know and love, but keep enough in liquid investments that you never have to sell the collection in a down market to pay bills.”
🔬 Go deeper — why natural resources diversify+

Supply of a resource is fixed in the short run and slow and costly to expand, so prices jump when demand outruns supply — for example during inflationary, high-rate periods that hurt stocks and bonds, or when emerging economies grow fast. That has historically given natural resources some negative correlation with financial assets.

Foreign markets are often more volatile, yet their low correlation with U.S. stocks, bonds and each other lowers portfolio risk. Accounting standards differ, so ratios may not compare. Foreign tax withheld can be taken as a foreign tax credit or an itemized deduction.

LO 5.5.2 · Exchange rates and dollar returns on foreign securities

−

Think of your money traveling. Before buying a foreign security, a strong dollar helps (it buys more foreign currency). While you hold it, you want the dollar to fall against the foreign currency, so the proceeds convert back into more dollars. The rule works both ways: any investor holding another country's securities wants the home currency to weaken during the holding period.

Reciprocal quote: foreign units per $1 = 1 ÷ (dollars per foreign unit)
Dollar cost = foreign price ÷ (foreign units per $1 at purchase)
Dollar proceeds = foreign sale value ÷ (foreign units per $1 at sale)
HPR in dollars = (proceeds − cost) ÷ cost
🧮 Worked example — U.K. shares in dollars

Buy shares for £8,000 when $1 = £0.80; sell for £8,400 when $1 = £0.75.

  2. Proceeds = £8,400 ÷ 0.75 = $11,200
  3. HPR = ($11,200 − $10,000) ÷ $10,000 = 12% (vs. 5% in pounds) — the dollar weakened, adding return
⚠️ Common wrong methods: reporting the local-currency return; multiplying where you should divide; assuming a stronger dollar helps an investor who already owns foreign shares.
Try it: Same trade, but at sale $1 = £0.84. Dollar HPR? (£8,400 ÷ 0.84 = $10,000 → 0%.)

−

  • Asset classes — cash & equivalents, fixed income, equities, real assets (real estate, collectibles, precious metals, stones)

🔬 Go deeper — other views of risk+

Besides loss of principal and return variability, the textbook lists price volatility, failing to earn the expected return, cash flows that don't match fixed living costs, and general uncertainty about future returns. A distorted view of risk leads either to a portfolio riskier than the client can tolerate or to one so cautious (e.g., all money market) that it cannot reach the goal. Perception of risk is often shaped by recent news and stories rather than actual probabilities, which pushes investors to buy after rises and sell after drops.

The risk-return pyramid ranks products from safest (insured deposits, Treasuries, savings bonds) up through high-grade bonds and stocks to speculative stocks, collectibles, limited partnerships, options and futures at the top.

LO 6.1.2 · Sources of risk: systematic vs. unsystematic

−
Total risk = unsystematic (diversifiable) + systematic (nondiversifiable)
Total risk → measured by standard deviation  |  systematic risk → measured by beta

Diversification can largely remove unsystematic risk, so systematic risk is the part investors should worry about most.

Unsystematic (one company, country or sector)

  • Business — uncertain operating income from the firm's (and sector's) practices
  • Financial — debt-vs-equity mix hurts the equity's value
  • Default (credit) — a debt issuer misses interest or principal; junk bonds most exposed; common stock not exposed
  • Political — a country's political/economic climate or law changes hurt the investment
  • Tax — uncertainty in tax law that could change returns
  • Investment manager — a fund manager's skill, philosophy, team or style changes
  • Liquidity — can't sell quickly without significant loss of principal (direct real estate, closely held firms)
  • Marketability — can't find a ready buyer

Systematic — PRIME (whole market)

  • Purchasing power — unexpected inflation; hits bonds, CDs, cash
  • Reinvestment rate — cash flows reinvested at lower rates; long bonds, GNMAs. Zero-coupon bonds eliminate it
  • Interest rate — bond prices move opposite to rates
  • Market — overall market volatility, from tangible events (wars, collapses) or intangible ones (speculation)
  • Exchange rate — currency moves cut a foreign holding's dollar return; Yankee bonds reduce it
🧮 Worked example — exchange rate risk

Theo, in the U.S., owns a Japanese bond paying ¥10,000 interest. At purchase $1 = ¥100; now $1 = ¥125.

  1. Then: ¥10,000 ÷ 100 = $100
  2. Now: ¥10,000 ÷ 125 = $80
  3. The dollar buys more yen, so it strengthened and Theo's dollar income fell $20.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying by the rate, or calling "more yen per dollar" a weaker dollar.
Try it: If the rate moved to $1 = ¥80 instead? (¥10,000 ÷ 80 = $125; the weaker dollar helps Theo by $25.)
⚖️ Commonly confused — business vs. financial; liquidity vs. marketability
PairFirstSecond
Business vs. financialOperations → operating incomeLeverage → capital structure
Liquidity vs. marketabilitySell quickly without a loss?Any buyer at all?
How to tell: ask "what is the source?" — how the firm operates vs. how it is financed; speed/price vs. existence of a market.
Exam trap: liquidity risk is unsystematic even though it sounds market-wide — it is not in PRIME. And a shareholder never faces default risk; a privately held company's shareholders do face liquidity risk.
Client conversation: "Some risks belong to one company — a bad CEO, too much debt. Owning many companies takes care of those. Other risks hit everything at once — inflation, rate moves, a market sell-off. We can't diversify those away, so we size how much of them you carry and keep you focused on the long run when markets get rough."
🔬 Go deeper — devaluation, revaluation and "liquid"+

When a country deliberately lowers its currency's value against another (usually the dollar), that is devaluation; raising it is revaluation. A weakening U.S. dollar benefits U.S. investors who own foreign securities.

"Liquid" is used loosely. Check whether the speaker means safety of principal, access to the money, a small loss on conversion to cash, or a small price concession when selling. Stocks carry some liquidity risk because their price can be below what you paid when you need to sell. Bonds with more default risk may also show more interest rate risk.

LO 6.2.2 · Mean, weighted return and standard deviation

−

Standard deviation is an absolute measure of how widely actual returns scatter around the mean — the most widely accepted measure of total risk.

Weighted average return: Rp = Σ (Wi × Ri)
Sample SD (exam default): S = √[ Σ(rt − r̄)2 ÷ (n − 1) ]
Population SD: σ = √[ Σ(rt − r̄)2 ÷ n ]
rt = return in period t; r̄ = mean return; n = number of observations
  • Mean — sum of observations ÷ count; best gauge of central tendency
  • Median — middle value when sorted low to high
  • Mode — most frequent value
🧮 Worked example — weighted return, then SD of five years

Nadia: $60,000 large-cap fund (11%), $25,000 bond fund (6%), $15,000 REIT (4%).

  1. Weighted return = 0.60(11) + 0.25(6) + 0.15(4) = 6.60 + 1.50 + 0.60 = 8.70%

A fund returned 12%, −4%, 9%, 15%, 3%.

  1. Mean = 35 ÷ 5 = 7%
  2. Deviations: 5, −11, 2, 8, −4 → squared: 25, 121, 4, 64, 16 → sum 230
  3. Sample variance = 230 ÷ 4 = 57.5 → S = √57.5 = 7.58%
  4. (Population: 230 ÷ 5 = 46 → σ = 6.78%)
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dividing by n for historical data; stopping at the variance; simple-averaging returns of unequal-sized holdings.
Try it: Returns 4%, 10%, 16%. Sample SD? (Mean 10; squares 36 + 0 + 36 = 72; 72 ÷ 2 = 36; S = 6%.)

LO 7.1.1 · Averaging returns: arithmetic vs. geometric mean

−

  • Geometric mean (GM) — the constant compound rate that turns $1 into the actual ending value; the preferred way to state a security's historical return.
AM = (r1 + r2 + … + rn) ÷ n
GM = [(1 + r1)(1 + r2)…(1 + rn)]1/n − 1
Calculator: PV = −1, FV = product of (1 + r), PMT = 0, N = n, solve I/YR.
🧮 Worked example — four years of fund returns

Returns: +10%, −5%, +18%, +7%.

  1. AM = (10 − 5 + 18 + 7) ÷ 4 = 7.50%.
  2. Product: 1.10 × 0.95 × 1.18 × 1.07 = 1.3194 (that is also the 4-year HPR of 31.94%).
  3. GM: PV −1, FV 1.3194, N 4 → 7.18%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: dropping the minus sign on a losing year; dividing the cumulative return by n and calling it the geometric mean (31.94 ÷ 4 = 7.98%).

LO 7.1.1 · Inflation, taxes and compounding: real return, after-tax return, APR vs. EAR

−
  • Nominal rate — stated rate before inflation (for a bond, its coupon rate); before tax for taxable investments.
  • Real rate — inflation-adjusted rate; use the exact formula, not subtraction.
  • APR — periodic rate × periods per year; no compounding.
  • EAR — the true annual rate once intra-year compounding is counted.
Real return = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1] × 100
After-tax return = nominal × (1 − marginal rate)  (federal + state if the state taxes it)
After-tax real = [(1 + after-tax) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1] × 100
EAR = (1 + i ÷ n)n − 1  where i = stated annual rate, n = compounding periods per year
🧮 Worked example — Priya's 7% bond

7% nominal coupon; inflation 2.5%; combined marginal tax rate 30%.

  1. Real (pre-tax): 1.07 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 4.39% (the shortcut 7 − 2.5 = 4.5% is close but not exact).
  2. After-tax: 7% × 0.70 = 4.90%.
  3. After-tax real: 1.049 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 2.34%.
  4. Card at 1.5%/month: APR = 18%; EAR = (1 + 0.18 ÷ 12)12 − 1 = 19.56%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying (1 + nominal)(1 + inflation); taxing the real return instead of the nominal return; reporting the monthly rate as the APR.

LO 7.2.1 · Information ratio and choosing the right measure

−

The information ratio grades an active manager: how much extra return was earned per unit of extra risk taken by straying from the benchmark.

IR = (RP − RB) ÷ σA
where RB = benchmark return (CAPM's required return may be used) and σA = standard deviation of the excess return (tracking error, “active risk”)
  • 0.50 or higher — often a top-quartile manager in its asset class.
  • Risk-free benchmark — IR reduces to the Sharpe ratio.
  • Negative alpha — IR becomes very hard to interpret; use it only when alpha is positive and beta is meaningful.
🧮 Worked example — two active managers

Benchmark 8%. Fund Q: 9.5% return, tracking error 3%. Fund R: 10% return, tracking error 6%.

  1. Q: (9.5 − 8) ÷ 3 = 0.50.
  2. R: (10 − 8) ÷ 6 = 0.33.
  3. Pick Q: less return, but far more efficient per unit of active risk.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using the fund's total standard deviation instead of tracking error; skipping the benchmark subtraction.
Try it: Fund K: 10%, σ 12%, β 0.9. Fund L: 13%, σ 20%, β 1.1. rf 3%, R² about 0.45. Which is better? R² < 0.70 → Sharpe only: K = 0.58, L = 0.50 → K (even though Treynor would favor L).

How to tell: “tracking error” or “active risk” in the question → information ratio.

LO 7.4.1 · Bond yields: current yield, YTM, YTC

−
Calculator defaults (unless told otherwise): FV = +$1,000 face; PMT = $1,000 × coupon ÷ 2; N = years × 2; 2 P/Yr; END mode; price = −PV. Semiannual applies to zero-coupon bonds too. The I/YR shown with 2 P/Yr is already annual.
Current yield = annual coupon interest ÷ current market price
YTM = solve I/YR with FV = par and N = periods to maturity
YTC = same, but FV = call price and N = periods to first call
  • YTM — the bond's IRR if held to maturity; assumes coupons are reinvested at the YTM itself.
  • Reinvestment risk — if rates drop, coupons are reinvested for less and the realized return falls short of the YTM. Zeros have none.
🧮 Worked example — discount and premium bonds

Bond A: 8 years, 5% coupon, price $940. Bond B: 10 years, 7% coupon, price $1,080, callable in 4 years at $1,020.

  1. A: CY = 50 ÷ 940 = 5.32%. YTM: PV −940, FV 1,000, PMT 25, N 16 → 5.95%.
  2. B: CY = 70 ÷ 1,080 = 6.48%. YTM: PV −1,080, FV 1,000, PMT 35, N 20 → 5.93%.
  3. B: YTC: PV −1,080, FV 1,020, PMT 35, N 8 → 5.21%.
  4. Zero: 10 years, price $600: PV −600, FV 1,000, PMT 0, N 20 → 5.17%.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using N = years with a semiannual PMT; reporting the semiannual rate without doubling; using par instead of the call price for YTC.
Try it: 15-year, 6% coupon, price $930, callable at par in 5 years. CY 6.45%; YTM 6.75%; YTC 7.71% → YTC > YTM > CY > coupon.
⚖️ Commonly confused — discount vs. premium ordering
BondTypical ordering
Discount (price < par)coupon < CY < YTM < YTC
Premium (price > par)coupon > CY > YTM > YTC
How to tell: a discount bond's gain to par comes sooner if called, so YTC is highest; a premium bond's loss to par comes sooner, so YTC is lowest. Exception: a premium call price well above par can lift YTC above YTM.
Exam trap: if the problem says the coupon is paid annually, switch to 1 P/Yr, PMT = full annual coupon and N = years.
Client conversation: “This bond shows a nice yield to maturity, but it's priced above face and can be called in four years. If that happens, you'd earn closer to 5.2%, so that's the number I'd count on.”

LO 7.4.1 · Taxable equivalent yield (TEY)

−

TEY converts a tax-free muni yield into the pre-tax yield a taxable bond would need to match it. Compare the TEY with the taxable bond's yield (same quality and maturity) — or, equivalently, compare after-tax yields.

Federal only (CFP Board formula): TEY = tax-exempt yield ÷ (1 − marginal rate)
Reverse: tax-free equivalent = taxable yield × (1 − marginal rate)
Double tax-exempt, itemizer: TEY = muni yield ÷ [(1 − SMTB)(1 − FMTB)] = muni yield ÷ (1 − [FMTB + SMTB(1 − FMTB)])
Double tax-exempt, non-itemizer: TEY = muni yield ÷ (1 − FMTB − SMTB)
🧮 Worked example — three investors
  1. 3.6% muni, 35% federal, no state tax: 3.6 ÷ 0.65 = 5.54%.
  2. Grace (itemizes), 3.4% in-state muni, 24% federal, 6% state: 3.4 ÷ (0.94 × 0.76) = 3.4 ÷ 0.7144 = 4.76%.
  3. Same facts but Grace doesn't itemize: 3.4 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 4.86%.
  4. Reverse: a 6% taxable bond at a 32% rate is worth 6 × 0.68 = 4.08% tax-free.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: multiplying the muni yield by (1 − t); dividing by t instead of (1 − t); comparing a pre-tax corporate yield directly with a tax-free yield.
Try it: Felix (32%, no state tax): 6% corporate or 4.3% muni? TEY 4.3 ÷ 0.68 = 6.32% > 6% → muni.
Exam trap: the Board's formula sheet uses federal tax only. Use the state version only when the question gives a state rate and says the bond is exempt from both.
Client conversation: “The muni's 4.3% looks lower, but you keep all of it. A taxable bond would have to pay about 6.3% to leave you the same amount after tax.”
🔬 Go deeper — why the itemizer formula isn't a simple sum+

For an itemizer, paying less state tax also means a smaller state-tax deduction on Schedule A, which nudges federal taxable income up. The term SMTB(1 − FMTB) captures that offset, so the combined effective rate is a little lower than the simple sum and the TEY a little lower than the non-itemizer figure.

LO 8.1.1 · MPT, the efficient frontier and indifference curves

−

Markowitz (1952) gave a way to measure how much diversification cuts risk. Risk = standard deviation. Unless two assets are perfectly positively correlated (+1.0), combining them lowers risk. MPT is about risk-adjusted return, not raw return: don't take more risk than the goal needs.

  • MPT assumptions — each investment is a probability distribution of returns over a holding period; risk = variability (standard deviation); decisions rest only on expected return and risk; investors maximize wealth over a set period (indifference fades beyond it); at equal risk, more return wins.
  • Mean-variance optimization — uses each asset's return, standard deviation, and its correlation with every other asset to build optimal portfolios.
  • Efficient frontier — the curve of portfolios with the most return for each level of risk.
  • Indifference curve — a picture of the investor's utility function: every point on one curve gives equal satisfaction. Curves slope upward and run parallel; higher curves = more satisfaction.
  • Optimal portfolio — where the highest reachable indifference curve is tangent to the frontier.
Position vs. frontierAttainable?Efficient?

🧮 Worked example — label three portfolios

At a 10% standard deviation the frontier's best expected return is 7%. Portfolio K: 5.5% / 10%. Portfolio L: 7% / 10%. Portfolio N: 9% / 10%.

  1. K sits below the frontier → attainable but inefficient (L beats it at the same risk).
  2. L sits on the frontier → attainable and efficient.
  3. N sits above the frontier → unattainable with these assets.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: calling K efficient because it can be built; calling N the "best" portfolio when it can't exist.
Try it: A fourth portfolio earns 7% at a 14% standard deviation. Efficient? (No — L earns 7% with less risk.)

How to tell: steeper = scared; the tangency point slides left as the curve steepens.
Exam trap: the optimal point is a tangency, not an intersection. A curve that cuts the frontier in two places gives lower utility than the tangent one; a curve wholly above the frontier is unreachable.
Client conversation: "For any amount of ups and downs you're willing to live with, there's a best mix you can build. We aim for that mix at your comfort level — not the mix with the highest return on paper."

LO 8.1.1 · Building portfolios on MPT principles

−

Few rules matter, yet many portfolios end up inefficient: too many funds, heavy overlap, and highly correlated holdings.

  • How many stocks? — many studies: 10–15 large-cap U.S. stocks across industries; others say 20–40 or more. Small/mid caps need about twice as many as large caps.
  • Overlap — several large-cap funds holding the same top names; the investor builds a pricey homemade index fund. Funds with huge holding counts add no further unsystematic-risk reduction and can dilute results.
  • Correlation — the key driver of lower portfolio standard deviation; aim for low correlations. Studies find 4–7 asset classes give sufficient diversification.
  • Correlation drift — figures are historical; U.S. and developed international markets have grown more correlated.
  • Adding new cash — a security that recently fell while the portfolio rose is negatively correlated with it and helps cut standard deviation.
  • Optimizer limits — output is very sensitive to estimated returns, standard deviations and correlations; it may drop asset classes clients want; adding min/max constraints means the output may no longer be optimal. Some advisors build intuitively, then check with software.
🧮 Worked example — spotting an overlap problem

Gina owns 11 funds: 7 large-cap U.S. funds (each 150+ holdings), 2 money market funds, 1 bond fund, 1 international fund.

  1. Seven large-cap funds ≈ 1,000+ positions, mostly the same top names → overlap, no added diversification.
  2. Only about three distinct asset classes are represented — below the 4–7 range.
  3. Fix: consolidate the large-cap sleeve into one or two funds; add lower-correlation classes (e.g., small caps, emerging markets, short-term bonds, real estate).
⚠️ Common wrong methods: assuming more funds = more diversification; counting funds instead of distinct, low-correlation asset classes.
Try it: How many small/mid-cap stocks would roughly match the diversification of 12 large caps? (About 24.)
Exam trap: standalone volatility doesn't decide whether an asset helps — its correlation with the existing portfolio does.
Client conversation: "Owning nine funds that all hold the same big companies is like buying the same stock nine times with nine fees. Let's own fewer things that actually behave differently."

LO 8.2.1 · Capital market theory and the capital market line

−

Sharpe extended Markowitz: pick the market portfolio M (all risky assets) on the frontier and draw a straight line from the risk-free rate through it. With a risk-free asset available, that line — the CML — replaces the curved frontier as the new efficient set.

CML: rp = rf + σp × [(rm − rf) ÷ σm]
where rp = portfolio expected return, rf = risk-free rate, rm = market return, σp and σm = standard deviations of the portfolio and the market.
  • Risk measure — standard deviation on the x-axis (like Markowitz's frontier).
  • Lending portfolio — between rf and M: some money in risk-free government securities; more conservative.
  • Borrowing portfolio — beyond M: margin/leverage; more aggressive.
  • Fully diversified — everything on the CML has correlation +1.0 with M and no unsystematic risk. Real portfolios (e.g., vs. the S&P 500) aren't like that, which is why the SML is needed.
🧮 Worked example — CML expected returns

rf = 3%, rm = 10%, σm = 16%. Slope = (10% − 3%) ÷ 16% = 0.4375.

  1. Lending portfolio, σp = 8%: 3% + 8% × 0.4375 = 6.5%
  2. Borrowing portfolio, σp = 20%: 3% + 20% × 0.4375 = 11.75%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: using rm instead of (rm − rf) in the slope; flipping σp/σm; plugging in beta.
Try it: Same market, σp = 4%. (3% + 4% × 0.4375 = 4.75%.)
Exam trap: the CML only prices efficient, fully diversified portfolios. Asked about an individual stock? Use the SML/CAPM with beta.
Client conversation: "Think of a dial between T-bills and the whole market. Turn it toward T-bills to calm things down, toward the market for more growth. Going past 100% market means borrowing — more upside and more downside."

LO 8.2.1 · CAPM and the security market line

−

CAPM is the practical result of capital market theory. It sets the return an asset should earn given its systematic risk (beta). The SML is CAPM drawn on a graph: intercept = rf, slope = market risk premium, x-axis = beta.

CAPM: ri = rf + (rm − rf)βi
Market risk premium = rm − rf (reward for investing in the market at all)
Stock risk premium = (rm − rf)βi (reward for owning that particular stock)
  • Only systematic risk is priced — unsystematic risk is assumed diversified away.
  • Assumptions — identical expectations; borrow and lend at the risk-free rate (13-week T-bill); no taxes or transaction costs; markets always in equilibrium.
  • Decision rule — CAPM return above the client's required return → positive decision; below → reject.
  • Equilibrium — expected and required returns are used interchangeably; all assets should plot on the SML.
  • Above the SML = undervalued (buy → price rises). Below = overvalued (sell/short → price falls).
  • Negative beta — moves against the market; investors accept a very low return for its diversification value.
  • Betas are historical — analysts often adjust them subjectively for expectations.
🧮 Worked example — required return and valuation

rf = 3%, rm = 9%, fund beta 1.3. The client's hurdle is 10%. An analyst forecasts the fund will return 9.5%.

  1. Market risk premium = 9% − 3% = 6%
  2. Stock risk premium = 6% × 1.3 = 7.8%
  3. CAPM required return = 3% + 7.8% = 10.8% → above the client's 10% hurdle, so the fund's risk/return fits her requirement
  4. But the 9.5% forecast is below the 10.8% the fund should earn → it plots below the SML → overvalued
⚠️ Common wrong methods: rm × β (forgets rf); stopping at the stock risk premium; dropping the minus sign on a negative beta.
Try it: Same market, beta 0.6. (3% + 6% × 0.6 = 6.6%.)

LO 8.2.1 · Arbitrage pricing theory (APT)

−

CAPM explains return with one factor (beta). APT is a multifactor alternative in which the market portfolio no longer plays the central role. Only unanticipated changes in factors matter — expected changes are already priced in.

APT: ri = a0 + b1F1 + b2F2 + … + bnFn + e
a0 = risk-free return; b = the security's sensitivity to each factor; F = risk premium for each unanticipated factor; e = error term.
  • Four primary factors — inflation; industrial production (GDP changes); risk premiums; yield curve (interest rates).
  • Sector vs. systematic influences — some factors hit one industry (banks vs. airlines); others hit all businesses (rates, overall activity).
  • Why CAPM is more common — APT's factor list isn't exhaustive, so results depend on the analyst and the factors chosen.
🧮 Worked example — APT expected return

Risk-free 2%. Inflation: b = 0.9, premium 4%. GDP: b = 1.1, premium 3%. Yield curve: b = 0.4, premium 2.5%.

  1. 0.9 × 4% = 3.6%
  2. 1.1 × 3% = 3.3%
  3. 0.4 × 2.5% = 1.0%
  4. 2% + 3.6% + 3.3% + 1.0% = 9.9%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: leaving out a0; adding raw premiums without weighting by sensitivity.
Try it: Raise the GDP sensitivity to 1.5. (2% + 3.6% + 4.5% + 1.0% = 11.1%.)
Exam trap: unemployment, trading volume and the like are not among the four primary APT factors (an analyst may still choose to use other factors).
Client conversation: "Some holdings react more to inflation surprises, others to growth or rate surprises. APT lets us see which surprises a holding is most exposed to."

LO 8.3.1 · The three forms of the efficient market hypothesis

−

Fama's EMH: prices reflect available information and adjust fast to news. News is by definition unexpected, so price changes are random (random walk) and no strategy beats the market consistently — the basis for buy-and-hold and index funds. Random walk does not mean prices are set randomly; it means future moves depend on information not yet known.

  • Efficient-market conditions — many competing participants; information readily available; tiny (or zero) transaction costs; no over- or undervalued securities; returns match risk taken.

WeakPast price and volume dataFundamental analysis, inside information
SemistrongAll public informationInside information only
StrongPublic + private informationNothing

Technical analysis has no value under any form. There is no “semiweak” form.

Weak-form testsLargely hold: technical rules don't consistently win

  • January effect — buy (especially small stocks) in December, sell in January; now showing up earlier as more investors exploit it; also seen abroad, so tax selling may not fully explain it.
  • Dividend-yield — higher payers have tended to beat lower payers.
  • Weekend effect — buy late Monday, sell late Friday; gains may not cover costs.
  • Unanticipated earnings — favorable surprises bring immediate price moves.
  • Low P/E — low-P/E stocks have tended to beat high-P/E stocks.
  • Size (small-firm) — small firms have outperformed, but with volatility that can be nearly double that of large firms.
  • BV/MV — high book-to-market stocks have outperformed (more likely undervalued).
  • Neglected firm — little analyst coverage → outperformance; possibly an offshoot of the size effect.
  • Value Line enigma — top-ranked (“1”) timeliness picks have done well, but frequent trading eats returns.

LO 8.3.2 · How the EMH relates to other investment approaches

−
  • Technical analysis — EMH says it doesn't work, but so many traders use it that support and resistance levels can steer short-term prices. Knowing them can help you time a buy near support rather than resistance.
  • Fundamental analysis — can add value only under the weak form. Disclosure rules and armies of analysts make an edge in big names hard to find.
  • Value anomaly — low-P/E, high-BV/MV firms often show weak ratios, so most analysts skip them. They get high required returns; as fortunes improve, those returns are realized. At times the SML even appears to slope the wrong way (lower risk, higher return) — the pattern value investors like most.
  • Active vs. passive — EMH points to index funds; well over half of mutual funds fail to beat their indexes. Strong managers tend to regress to the mean; staying in the top 25% of an asset class consistently is rare.
  • Brinson, Hood & Beebower — more than 90% of the variability of portfolio returns came from asset allocation; selection and timing explained little (the conclusions were later debated).
  • Foreign markets — efficiency depends on information. Developed foreign markets use accounting standards that differ from U.S. GAAP, rewarding analysts who adjust. Emerging and frontier markets have less reliable statements; managers lean on top-down country views and local contacts.
  • Other markets — real estate, art and tangibles have limited, narrow information: EMH doesn't apply everywhere.
🧮 Worked example — splitting index vs. active by efficiency

Ana has $400,000 for equities: 70% U.S. large cap, 30% emerging markets.

  1. Large cap ($280,000): highly efficient → low-cost index fund.
  2. Emerging markets ($120,000): less efficient → active manager may add value.
⚠️ Common wrong methods: paying for active management in the most efficient segment; avoiding inefficient markets entirely.
Try it: Which of her sleeves would a strict EMH believer index? (Both.)
Exam trap: Brinson measured the variability of returns, not the level of returns.
Client conversation: "Most of how your portfolio behaves comes from the mix of stocks, bonds and cash — not from picking hot funds. So we spend our energy on the mix and keep costs low where markets are efficient."

LO 8.4.1 · Risk tolerance, Monte Carlo and the investment policy statement

−

Good investment counseling reviews goals, risk tolerance and exposure, taxes, liquidity/marketability needs, and financial statements. Measuring risk tolerance remains more art than science.

  • Loss aversion — a gamble is preferred over a sure loss of equal expected value; e.g., holding a loser until it gets back to breakeven. Losses hurt more than equal gains please (Kahneman & Tversky).
  • Risk aversion — a sure gain is preferred over a gamble of equal expected value (the “sleep at night” test). Tolerance looks higher in rising markets → buy high, sell low.
  • Liquidity available — high liquidity and job stability support more risk.
  • Proximity/priority of goals — goals within ~5 years: low-risk assets; longer: stocks can fit.
  • Psychographics — methodical/cautious types are more risk averse; individualistic/spontaneous types less so.
  • Monte Carlo simulation — stochastic modeling with thousands of random trials to give probabilities of outcomes. Pros: ranks paths best to worst, models taxes and asset location, contributions/withdrawals, short- vs. long-term risk, “Can I retire earlier?” Cons: simplistic historical inputs; simulating an index (e.g., Wilshire 5000) instead of fee-bearing funds can overstate results.

LO 8.5.1 · Investments across interest-rate and economic cycles

−

Investment planning matches vehicles to goals. Start with an emergency fund; without one, limit volatile assets (stocks) and illiquid ones (real estate). If goals are unrealistic for the client's finances, revise them; count expected resources (bonus, inheritance) only if fairly certain.

  • Top-down view — next economic phase → Fed policy → money supply and rates → stock and bond prices. (MPT leans on fundamental analysis, valid under the weak form.)
  • Rates up — bonds fall; stocks generally fall too. Rates down → both tend to rise. Financials and high-debt firms are hit harder than nondurable-goods makers.
  • Staying flexible — money market funds and short-term Treasuries let you reinvest as rates keep rising.
  • Cycle — expansion: stocks up, Fed may raise rates → bonds down; contraction: stocks down, Fed may cut → bonds up. Not clear-cut; they can move together.
  • Inflation — rising inflation nearly always lifts rates and cuts bond prices; moderate inflation may not hurt stocks; strong inflation hurts most industries, but commodity-linked areas (oil and gas, real estate, natural resources) may gain.
  • Company life cycle — development → growth → expansion (growth slowing, competition rising) → maturity (growth ≈ GDP) → decline.
  • Sensitivity analysis — recompute NPV and IRR under pessimistic, expected, and optimistic assumptions, changing one input at a time, to see how reliable the outcome is.

LO 8.5.2 · Asset allocation methodologies

−

Asset allocation spreads wealth across countries and asset classes and is the main driver of long-run performance (about 90% of the variability, per studies). Most planners use mean-variance optimization software. Build the model around risk and time horizon, investing experience, required return, and tax status.

  • Four policy decisions — which asset classes; what weights; allowable ranges; which specific securities.
  • Time-horizon imbalance — retirees over-weighting bonds face inflation erosion; young investors over-weighting stocks may bail in a bear market.
  • Model portfolios — questionnaire into a “black box.” Limits: not customized; one-size-fits-all with assumptions the advisor may not know; benchmark relevance; poor tax handling. Aim for tax efficiency (max after-tax income), not an all-muni portfolio.
  • Strategic — long-term mix from goals, risk tolerance and life-cycle phase (accumulation → conservation/protection → distribution/gifting); held until a life event; rebalanced back to target. Lifecycle (target-date) 401(k) funds are an example.
  • Tactical (TAA) — continuously shifts to exploit perceived mispricing; market timing; contrarian (buys out-of-favor classes); higher costs; little weight on goals.
  • Choosing — active believers in inefficient markets lean TAA; passive buy-and-hold investors lean strategic. Sticking with the chosen method matters most.
  • Core and satellite — passive index core (U.S. stocks, U.S. bonds, developed international) + higher-risk satellites (REITs, emerging markets, high-yield). Core = strategic, satellite = tactical.
  • Value vs. growth — value bets the P/E is too low and will re-rate (price focus); growth bets earnings will rise with P/E steady (earnings focus; ~15%+ growth, low payout, high P/E, higher beta).

LO 8.6.1 · Crisis events and adapting to new norms

−

Plans usually cover personal shocks (job loss, business failure, death). A market-wide crisis hits everyone — advisor included — so staying level-headed and objective matters most.

  • Black swan — rare, unpredictable, severe, beyond normal volatility; seems predictable only afterward; models can't forecast it or its full impact.
  • Endogenous risk — from inside the financial system, amplified by herding (1929 crash, 2008–2009 crisis).
  • Exogenous risk — outside shocks (terror attack, earthquake, tsunami, pandemic) that slow commerce and spread fear.
  • Feedback loop — monitoring flows back to data gathering, analysis and goals, because client situations change gradually or suddenly.
  • Liquidity — regular access to liquid assets (e.g., money market account) to absorb short-term setbacks.
  • Government relief — stimulus checks, enhanced unemployment, tax credits, business loans helped during COVID-19; know the rules and tax effects, but never assume they'll be repeated.
  • Long-term positive bias — over time the odds favor the patient, diversified investor; time is the single most powerful factor. Policy must transcend current conditions; micromanaging brings trading losses, costs, and missing early rebounds.

Decision 1 · LO 8.4.1 · IPS: liquidity and risk tolerance

−

The IPS sets objectives, the allocation policy, management procedures and communication. Its core content: return requirement, risk tolerance, liquidity, time horizon, laws and regulations, taxes, unique circumstances, and permitted/excluded investments.

Decision 2 · LO 8.6.1 · LO 1.3.1 · LO 7.1.1 · Reserves and crisis response

−

Client conversation: “Bexley earned more, but it took much bigger swings to do it. Per unit of risk, Alder did better, and it costs a fraction as much.”
🔬 Go deeper — why low R² breaks Treynor and Jensen+

Both use beta, which captures only market-related risk. When R² is low, most of the fund’s movement comes from other factors, so beta misstates its risk. Sharpe uses total risk (standard deviation) and works for any portfolio.

Decision 5 · Taxes: wash sales, TEY and placement (LO 1.4.1 · LO 7.4.1 · LO 3.6.1)

−

Three tax tools: avoid wash sales when harvesting losses, compare municipals on a taxable-equivalent basis, and place tax-inefficient holdings in tax-deferred accounts.

  • Wash sale — loss sale plus purchase of the same or similar securities within 30 days before or after (2026 edition figure): loss postponed and added to the new basis.
  • Net capital loss limit — $3,000 a year against other income, remainder carried forward (2026 edition figure).
  • Placement — taxable interest, nonqualified dividends, high turnover, taxable zeros → tax-deferred; index funds and munis → taxable.
TEY (federal only): tax-free yield ÷ (1 − federal rate)
TEY (federal + state exempt, itemizer): tax-free yield ÷ [(1 − state)(1 − federal)]
Non-itemizer: divide by 1 − (federal + state).
🧮 Worked solution — Q17

Sold Harborline for $85,000 (basis $100,000); bought it back 21 days later for $82,000.

  1. Realized loss: $85,000 − $100,000 = −$15,000.
  2. Repurchase within 30 days → recognized loss $0.
  3. New basis: $82,000 + $15,000 = $97,000. Answer (Q17): $0 loss; basis $97,000
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Treating the loss as gone for good; applying the $3,000 cap here.
🧮 Worked solution — Q18 and Q19

  3. Same mix, less tax: bonds into the 401(k), stock index funds or munis in the joint account. Answer (Q19): Hold the taxable bond exposure in the 401(k) and keep tax-efficient index funds or municipals in the joint account

Decision 6 · Bond risk and the college money (LO 4.1.2 · LO 2.3.1)

−

Duration measures a bond’s price sensitivity to rates and should line up with the goal’s time horizon. A bond fund never matures, so its price risk cannot be held away.

Price change: ΔP ÷ P = −D × [Δy ÷ (1 + y)]
where D = duration, Δy = change in rates, y = current YTM (not the coupon)
  • Immunization — portfolio duration = time horizon, so price risk and reinvestment risk offset.
  • Zero-coupon bond — duration equals maturity; accrued interest is taxed yearly in a taxable account (phantom income).
🧮 Worked solution — Q20

Ridgeway: $60,000, duration 6.2, YTM 5.2%, rates +1%.

  1. Δy ÷ (1 + y) = 0.01 ÷ 1.052 = 0.009506.
  2. −6.2 × 0.009506 = −5.89%.
  3. $60,000 × (1 − 0.0589) ≈ $56,464. Answer (Q20): $56,464
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Rule of thumb only ($56,280); wrong sign ($63,536).
Try it: What if rates fell 0.5%? −6.2 × (−0.005 ÷ 1.052) = +2.95% → about $61,768.
🧮 Worked solution — Q21

Leah’s $96,000 is in stocks; tuition due in 3 years.

  1. Money needed within 5 years should generally leave stocks.
  2. Match duration to 3 years: a 3-year Treasury zero has duration 3 and no default or reinvestment risk. Answer (Q21): Three-year Treasury zero-coupon bonds, whose duration equals the three-year horizon
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Reaching for yield with a long-duration fund; a barbell for a dated goal.
Exam trap: In the duration formula, y is the current yield to maturity, not the coupon rate.
Client conversation: “Leah’s money has a due date, so we buy something that comes due on that date. Then rate moves and market drops can’t change what she gets.”

Decision 7 · Currency and the European fund (LO 5.5.2)

−

A foreign holding’s dollar return combines its local return with the currency move. Think of the money traveling: it goes abroad at purchase and comes home at sale.

Dollar HPR: (1 + local return) × (ending $ per unit ÷ beginning $ per unit) − 1
🧮 Worked solution — Q22

$36,000 invested at €1 = $1.08; euro value +9%; now €1 = $1.02.

  1. $36,000 ÷ 1.08 = €33,333.
  2. €33,333 × 1.09 = €36,333.
  3. €36,333 × $1.02 = $37,060.
  4. ($37,060 − $36,000) ÷ $36,000 = 2.94%. Answer (Q22): 2.94%
⚠️ Common wrong methods: Reporting 9% (local only); subtracting the currency loss (3.44%); inverting the rate (15.41%).
Try it: If the euro had risen to $1.12 instead: 1.09 × (1.12 ÷ 1.08) − 1 = 13.04%.
Exam trap: A U.S. investor in foreign securities wants the dollar to fall during the holding period; a stronger dollar reduces the return.
Client conversation: “Your European fund did well in euros, but the euro lost ground to the dollar, so you kept about 3% of that 9%. That currency swing is part of the diversification you’re buying.”

Decision tree — which performance measure?

−
  1. Is R² with the benchmark below 0.70 (or is this the client's whole, undiversified portfolio)? → beta is unreliable → Sharpe (total risk).
  2. Diversified, R² ≥ 0.70, ranking several managers? → Treynor (return per unit of beta).
  3. Need "did it beat what its risk required, in % points?" → Jensen's alpha (absolute).
  4. Judging active management against a stated benchmark? → information ratio.
Exam trap: Sharpe and Treynor only rank portfolios against each other; alpha is the one that is meaningful by itself.

Decision tree — which bond yield or strategy?

−
Quoting yield
  • Callable and trading at a premium → compare YTM and YTC; quote the lower.
  • Comparing a muni with a taxable bond → convert to taxable-equivalent yield at the client's marginal rate.
  • Need cash income per dollar today → current yield.
Strategy
  • A known future liability (tuition, a date) → immunize: match duration (not maturity) to the horizon; a zero maturing on the date does it alone.
  • Passive, steady cash rolling to the long end → ladder.
  • Active: short end for liquidity, long end to lock rates → barbell.
  • One cash need on one date, bought over time → bullet.
