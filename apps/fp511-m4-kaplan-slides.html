HabitClear memory (DOWNSHIFT, C ALL) before every new problem so old entries don't contaminate the next answer.

🔢Five values — you need at least three to solve

Every TVM problem is built from these five registers. Enter any three (sometimes four) and solve for the missing one.

The five TVM registers
KeyRegisterNotes
PVPresent valueUsually a negative input when a FV is also in the problem
FVFuture valueUsually a positive input
PMTPaymentNegative if a cash outflow, positive if a cash inflow
NNumber of periodsMust match the compounding frequency

Exam trap — the sign keyThe +/− key (above the blue key) makes a number negative — enter the number then press +/−. Do not mistake it for the subtraction − key (above +). Sign convention: outflows negative, inflows positive.

Future value (FV)

📈FV of a single amount

FV is the value a lump sum grows to over a given period at a specified rate. Compounding = interest earned on an increasing balance of principal and prior interest.

Problem — Brenden. He buys an antique chair today for $20,000; experts say it will grow 5% annually for the next five years. About what is it worth at the end of year 5?

FV of a single amount — END mode

1

DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → 20000, +/−, PV
2
Enter the rate and term.
5, I/YR → 5, N
3

FV = 25,525.6313, or ≈ $25,526

💰FV of an annuity

Used when a systematic savings program accumulates funds toward a future goal. An annuity = equal, regular payments. Annuity due pays at the beginning of each period (e.g., a lease); an ordinary annuity pays at the end (e.g., a mortgage).

Present value (PV)

📉PV of a single amount

PV is the value today of an amount to be received in the future, discounted over a number of periods at a given rate. The further out the dollar and/or the higher the discount rate, the lower the PV.

Number of compounding periods (N) & the Rule of 72

⏱️Solving for N

N can be the number of years for a PV to grow to a FV, or the number of years for payments to grow to a FV.

Problem — Connor. He invested $8,000 several years ago; today it's worth $15,000, earning a 12% annual compound return. Approximately how many years was it invested?

Solve for N — END mode

1
Clear; enter the original amount as a negative PV.
DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → 8000, +/−, PV
2
Enter today's value and the rate.
15000, FV → 12, I/YR
3
Solve for N.
N
N = 5.55 years

✌️The Rule of 72 — the doubling shortcut

The Rule of 72 estimates either the number of years for a single amount to double, or the interest rate needed to double within a set number of years. To find the years to double, divide 72 by the annual interest rate.

Problem — Madelyn. She puts all her cash into an account earning 10%, compounded annually, with no additions or withdrawals. In about how many years will she double her money?

Exact TVM calculation vs. the Rule of 72
MethodSetupResult
TVM calculationPV = 1 · FV = 2 · i = 10 · PMT = 0 → solve N7.3 years
Rule of 7272 ÷ 107.2 years
Exam trapThe Rule of 72 gives an approximate number of years only — the exact TVM answer (7.3) differs slightly from the shortcut (7.2).

Learning Objective 4.2.1

Loan payment & amortization

🏠How amortization works

Amortization = repayment of loan principal over time. An amortization schedule shows how much of each payment goes to principal vs. interest.

  • Early payments — more interest, less principal
  • Over time — the principal portion rises and the interest portion falls
Matching the periodFor monthly loans, set 12 P/YR and enter years with the xP/YR key (DOWNSHIFT, N) so the calculator converts to the correct number of monthly periods.

A $150,000 mortgage at 3.25% annual interest over 15 years. They want a status check after 24 months: how much principal have they paid, and how much interest?

Learning Objective 4.2.2

Inflation-adjusted interest rate

🔥The real (inflation-adjusted) rate

It is not simply the difference between the rate of return and inflation — it discounts the return by the inflation rate. Used to calculate the amount needed when a payment is simultaneously compounding (serial payments); if not used, the result will be inaccurate, and the error grows as the number of payments and the return-vs-inflation gap increase.

Formula
[ (1 + rate of return) ÷ (1 + rate of inflation) − 1 ] × 100

Question. What is the inflation-adjusted rate with a 7% rate of return and a 3% inflation rate?

Apply the formula

1
Divide the growth factors.
(1.07 ÷ 1.03) − 1
2
Subtract 1, then multiply by 100.
(1.0388 − 1) × 100 = 0.03883 × 100
Inflation-adjusted rate = 3.8835, or 3.88%
Exam trapThe naïve subtraction (7% − 3% = 4%) is wrong. The real rate here is 3.88%, not 4% — always use the ratio formula.

Learning Objective 4.2.3

Serial (inflation-adjusted) payments

📶Payments that rise with inflation

Serial payments increase each year by inflation to keep a constant real (purchasing-power) amount.

  • The initial serial payment is less than the equivalent fixed (equal) payment
  • The last serial payment is greater than the last fixed payment — but it holds the same purchasing power as the first serial payment

Problem — Ty & Skylar. They want to buy a beach house five years from today and know they must accumulate at least $150,000 in today's dollars. They expect a 10% after-tax return and 4% inflation, making equal inflation-adjusted payments at the end of each year. What serial payments occur at the end of years 2 and 5?

Step 1 — base payment at the inflation-adjusted rate (END mode)

1
Clear; enter goal, PV, and the real rate.
DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → END mode → FV = 150000 → PV = 0
2
Use the inflation-adjusted rate and term; solve PMT.
i = 5.7692 [((1.10 ÷ 1.04) − 1) × 100] → n = 5 → PMT = −$26,732.35
Base serial payment = $26,732.35

Step 2 — inflate each year by 4%

1
Year 1: $26,732.35 × 1.04 = $27,801.64
2
Year 2: $27,801.64 × 1.04 = $28,913.71
3
Year 3: $28,913.71 × 1.04 = $30,070.26
4
Year 4: $30,070.26 × 1.04 = $31,273.07
5
Year 5: $31,273.07 × 1.04 = $32,523.99
End of year 2 = $28,913.71 · End of year 5 = $32,523.99

Learning Objective 4.3.1

Internal rate of return (IRR)

🧮Cash-flow keystroke rules

  • Cash inflows are positive; cash outflows are negative
  • Determine the compounding period first — it's the time between two consecutive cash flows (semiannual = two periods/yr)
  • Enter a cash flow — or a zero — for every compounding period in the holding period
  • The first outflow (usually the purchase) is CF0 at N = 0; for an NPV it is entered as the initial outlay
  • Equal consecutive cash flows can be grouped with Nj (DOWNSHIFT on the CFj key)

Problem — antique chair. Bought six years ago for $1,000, repaired at the end of year 2 for $450, just sold for $2,850. What is the average compound rate earned (IRR)?

Solve for IRR — END mode, 1 P/YR

1
Clear; enter CF0 (purchase outflow).
DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → END mode, 1 P/YR → 1000, +/−, CFj
2
Year 1 zero, year 2 repair outflow.
0, CFj → 450, +/−, CFj
3
Group the three zero years, then the sale inflow.
0, CFj → 3, DOWNSHIFT, Nj → 2850, CFj
4
Solve for IRR/YR.
DOWNSHIFT, IRR/YR
IRR = 13.2502, or 13.25%

📆IRR with quarterly cash flows

Problem. A three-year mutual-fund investment pays quarterly distributions (not reinvested): four at $500, four at $570, and four at $600. The initial investment was $120,000, and the account's final value at the last quarterly distribution was $165,000. What IRR was earned?

Solve for a quarterly IRR, then annualize — END mode, 1 P/YR

1
Clear; enter the initial investment as CF0.
DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → END mode, 1 P/YR → 120000, +/−, CFj
2
Group the equal quarterly distributions with Nj.
500, CFj → 4, DOWNSHIFT, Nj → 570, CFj → 4, DOWNSHIFT, Nj → 600, CFj → 3, DOWNSHIFT, Nj
3
Final flow = last $600 distribution + $165,000 value.
165600, CFj
4
Solve for the periodic IRR, then × 4 to annualize.
DOWNSHIFT, IRR/YR = 3.0894 × 4
IRR = 3.0894 × 4 = 12.3577, or 12.36%
Watch the last flowThe 12th distribution ($600) is folded into the final cash flow ($600 + $165,000 = $165,600), so only three $600 flows are grouped with Nj. The periodic (quarterly) IRR is multiplied by 4 to get the annual rate.

Learning Objective 4.3.2

PV & net present value (NPV) of uneven cash flows

📈What NPV tells you

NPV evaluates capital projects and long-term investments. It is the difference between the total PV of the cash flows and the initial outlay (cost).

Reading the NPV result
NPVMeaning
PositiveInvestment earns more than the discount (required) rate
NegativeInvestment earns less than the discount rate

PV of cash flows − cost of investment = NPV of investment.

Problem — Abby. Equipment costs $100,000; she can sell it for $40,000 at the end of year 5. Over five years it adds annual cash flows of $25,000, $30,000, $20,000, $15,000, and $10,000. At an 8% opportunity cost, what is the NPV?

Solve for NPV

1
Clear; enter the cost as CF0 (year 0).
DOWNSHIFT, C ALL → 100000, +/−, CFj
2
Enter years 1–4 cash flows.
25000, CFj → 30000, CFj → 20000, CFj → 15000, CFj
3
Year 5 = final $10,000 + $40,000 sale price.
50000, CFj
4
Enter the discount rate and solve NPV.
8, I/YR → DOWNSHIFT, NPV
NPV = 9,799.5652, or $9,799.57 (positive → beats the 8% hurdle)
