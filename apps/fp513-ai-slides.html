🧾Worked example — Harvesting a loss without tripping the wash sale rule

Fictional client Mara has a $12,000 net long-term gain this year from selling a fund. She also holds 300 shares of Pine Co. with a $21,000 basis now worth $15,600.

  1. Sell Pine: $15,600 − $21,000 = −$5,400 short-term loss (held under a year).
  2. Cross-net: $12,000 LT gain − $5,400 = $6,600 net LT gain taxed at 0/15/20% (2026 edition figure) instead of $12,000.
  3. If she rebuys Pine within 30 days before or after the sale (2026 edition figure), the loss is disallowed and her new basis becomes the repurchase cost + $5,400.
  4. Option: wait 31+ days, or hold something similar but not the same meanwhile.

✅Must know

TrapA barbell is active; a ladder is the passive, staggered one

🧾Worked example — Convertible bond: five numbers from one set of facts

$1,000 par, 4% semiannual coupon, 10 years left, straight bonds yield 6%, conversion price $62.50, stock $55, convertible priced at $1,000.

  • Conversion ratio = $1,000 ÷ $62.50 = 16 shares
  • Conversion value = 16 × $55 = $880
  • Investment value: N 20, I/YR 3, PMT $20, FV $1,000 → $851.23
  • Floor = greater of $851.23 and $880 = $880
  • Conversion premium = $120 ÷ $880 = 13.64%
  • Investment premium = $148.77 ÷ $851.23 = 17.48%
  • Downside risk = $148.77 ÷ $1,000 = 14.88%

🧾Worked example — Dollar cost averaging: average cost vs. average price

Jun invests $3,000 a month for four months at prices of $20, $15, $24 and $16.

1$20150.0

3$24125.0

Average cost = $12,000 ÷ 662.5 = $18.11. Average price = ($20 + $15 + $24 + $16) ÷ 4 = $18.75. Bond & Stock Valuation

🧮Key numbers & formulas

Duration price change
ΔP/P = −D × [Δy ÷ (1 + y)]; y = current YTM (semiannual: y ÷ 2)
Rule of thumb
%ΔP ≈ −D × Δy (duration 6, +1% → about −6%)
Bond price inputs
N = years × 2, PMT = coupon ÷ 2, I/YR = market yield, FV = par
Sustainable growth
g = ROE × (1 − payout)
Gordon model
V = D0(1 + g) ÷ (r − g); g must be < r
No-growth / preferred
V = D ÷ r
PEG
P/E ÷ g (whole number); under 1.00 = value
P/FCF
(1 + g) ÷ (r − g)

✅Must know

  • Duration falls as coupon or market yield rises and grows with maturity; a zero's duration equals its maturity
  • Match a bond's duration, not its maturity, to the client's time horizon
  • Positive convexity: duration understates gains and overstates losses; callable and mortgage-backed bonds show negative convexity
  • Premium bond: CY > YTM > YTC; discount bond: CY < YTM < YTC
  • Leverage raises ROE and EPS but adds fixed interest that hurts in a contraction; low ROA vs. ROE signals leverage
  • Constant growth fits mature firms; multistage fits growth-phase firms; FCFE fits non-dividend payers
  • Intrinsic value above price = undervalued; relative methods need a peer or industry benchmark

⚠️Traps & tips

TrapUsing the coupon rate as y in the duration formula
TrapPlugging D0 into the Gordon model without growing it to D1
TrapMultiplying ROE by the payout ratio instead of the retention rate
TrapTreating technical analysis as useful under any form of the EMH
TrapPicking the lowest P/E without adjusting for growth (use PEG)
Exam tipPredict premium or discount before pricing a bond; it catches calculator entry errors
Exam tipIf a YTM is given in a duration question, use the (1 + y) version of the formula
Exam tipIn multistage problems, add the terminal value to the last high-growth year's cash flow before taking the NPV

🧾Worked example — Is Kestrel worth $54 to Selma?

Kestrel just paid a $2.40 dividend, expected to grow 4% a year. Selma requires 9%.

  1. D1 = 2.40 × 1.04 = $2.496
  2. V = 2.496 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = $49.92
  3. Price $54 > value $49.92 → overvalued for Selma; don't buy at $54.

Sensitivity: if her required return fell to 8%, V = 2.496 ÷ 0.04 = $62.40 — small changes in r − g swing the answer a lot.

✅Must know

  • Max gain/loss for all four option positions: only the long call (gain) and naked short call (loss) are unlimited
  • Intrinsic value: call = stock − strike, put = strike − stock, never below zero; time value = premium − intrinsic
  • Black-Scholes: every input raises call value except the exercise price
  • Short hedge = own the asset, sell futures; long hedge = need the asset, buy futures
  • REIT income is portfolio income; RELPs fall under passive activity rules

⚠️Traps & tips

TrapSubtracting mortgage interest or depreciation when computing NOI
TrapCalling a long put's gain unlimited (it is strike − premium)
TrapRestoring a futures account only to maintenance margin
TrapHedging a short sale with puts instead of calls
TrapChoosing a global fund to diversify away from U.S. holdings
Exam tipWrite the four-position payoff table from memory before any option question
Exam tipFor currency questions, first restate the quote as foreign units per $1, then divide to get dollars
Exam tipHedges always take the position opposite the cash exposure

🧾Worked example — Collar on a concentrated position

Rina owns 1,000 shares bought at $20, now $60. She buys 10 puts with a $55 strike at $2.40 and writes 10 calls with a $70 strike at $2.40.

  • Net premium: $2,400 received − $2,400 paid = $0 (zero-cost collar)
  • Floor: if the stock falls to $40, she can sell at $55 → value protected at $55,000
  • Ceiling: above $70 the shares are called away at $70 → value capped at $70,000
  • Range kept until expiration: $55,000 to $70,000, versus $60,000 today

Investment Risk & Quantitative Concepts

📊Key numbers & formulas

68 / 95 / 99%
Share of returns within ±1, 2, 3 SD (textbook uses 99%, not 99.7%)
R² ≥ 0.70
Beta reliable; Treynor/Jensen OK (Morningstar). ρ ≈ ±0.84
10–15
Large-cap stocks in different industries to diversify (exam rule)
25–30 / 4–7
Small/mid-cap stocks / mutual funds in different asset classes
n − 1
Sample SD divisor for historical returns (calculator Sx)
β = 1.0
Market; <1 defensive, >1 aggressive, 0 = no correlation (T-bills)
ρ = +1
Only case where portfolio SD = weighted-average SD

✅Must know

  • Total risk (SD) = systematic (beta) + unsystematic; PRIME = purchasing power, reinvestment rate, interest rate, market, exchange rate
  • β = COVim ÷ σm2 = ρimσi ÷ σm; portfolio beta = dollar-weighted average
  • CV = σ ÷ mean; lower is better
  • COV = ρσiσj; ρ = COV ÷ (σiσj); R2 = ρ2; 1 − R2 = unsystematic share
  • σp = √[Wi2σi2 + Wj2σj2 + 2WiWjCOVij]
  • Normal distribution for annual returns; lognormal for ending portfolio value

⚠️Traps & tips

TrapLow beta ≠ low volatility — low correlation shrinks beta (gold funds)
TrapLiquidity and marketability risk are unsystematic; common stock has no default risk
TrapDon't weight-average SDs unless ρ = +1; don't stop at the variance
TrapOnly one tail: 95% within ±2 SD leaves 2.5% above and 2.5% below
TrapCV questions pad the facts — ignore beta and R2
Exam tipCheck R2 first: it tells you whether to trust beta (Treynor/Jensen) or use SD (Sharpe)
Exam tipCorrelation pyramid: covariance (unbounded) → correlation (−1 to +1) → R2 (square it)
Exam tipZero-coupon bonds eliminate reinvestment rate risk; Yankee bonds reduce exchange rate risk

🧾Worked example — Is the new fund a real diversifier?

Rosa holds an S&P 500 index fund. A candidate fund has σ = 20, the market σ = 16, and their covariance is 224.

  1. ρ = 224 ÷ (20 × 16) = 0.70
  2. R2 = 0.702 = 0.49 → under 0.70, so it is not just an index look-alike, and beta (224 ÷ 256 = 0.875) is not reliable
  3. Judge it with standard deviation and Sharpe, and check portfolio SD: at 50/50 with this correlation, √[0.25(400) + 0.25(256) + 2(0.25)(224)] = √276 ≈ 16.61, below the 18.00 weighted-average SD.

✅Must know

  • Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 — not subtraction
  • Geometric mean < arithmetic mean unless all returns are equal
  • Time-weighted = the investment/manager; dollar-weighted = the investor's IRR
  • Alpha = actual − CAPM required; Treynor ÷ beta; Sharpe ÷ standard deviation
  • YTC: FV = call price, N = periods to first call
  • TEY = muni yield ÷ (1 − t); in-state itemizer: ÷ (1 − SMTB)(1 − FMTB)

⚠️Traps & tips

TrapUsing Treynor/Jensen when R² is below 0.70
TrapForgetting to double the semiannual rate (or using N = years)
TrapMultiplying a muni yield by (1 − t) instead of dividing
TrapBenchmarking a multi-asset portfolio against the S&P 500 alone
TrapTreating a multi-year HPR as an annual return
Exam tipDiscount bond: coupon < CY < YTM < YTC; premium bond: the reverse
Exam tipIn Sharpe/Treynor/IR you can use whole numbers or decimals — just be consistent
Exam tipCompare Sharpe ratios only within one asset class

🧾Worked example — Did the manager add value?

Portfolio: 11% return, beta 1.2, σ 16%, R² 0.85. Market 9%, risk-free 3%.

  • CAPM required = 3 + 1.2(9 − 3) = 10.2% → alpha +0.80%
  • Treynor = (11 − 3) ÷ 1.2 = 6.67
  • Sharpe = (11 − 3) ÷ 16 = 0.50

R² ≥ 0.70, so all three are usable; positive alpha means the manager beat the return required for the risk taken.

Portfolio Theory & Asset Allocation

🧭Key numbers & formulas

CAPM
r = rf + (rm − rf)β
CML
rp = rf + σp(rm − rf) ÷ σm
APT
r = a0 + b1F1 + … + bnFn + e (4 primary factors)
Diversification
10–15 large caps (some studies 20–40); ~2× as many small/mid caps; 4–7 asset classes
Brinson et al.
>90% of return variability from asset allocation
Liquidity rule of thumb
Money needed within ~5 years stays out of stocks
Growth stocks
Earnings growth ~15%+/yr, low payout, high P/E, higher beta

✅Must know

  • Optimal portfolio = tangency of the highest indifference curve with the efficient frontier; steeper curve = more risk averse
  • CML uses standard deviation (efficient portfolios); SML uses beta (any security); above SML = undervalued
  • Weak form: fundamental + inside info can win; semistrong: inside info only; strong: nothing; technical analysis never
  • IPS: return and risk objectives plus liquidity, time horizon, legal, tax, unique circumstances, permitted/excluded investments
  • Strategic = long-term mix + rebalancing; TAA = contrarian market timing; core-and-satellite = strategic core + tactical satellite
  • Crisis: ignore noise, watch incremental change, rewrite the IPS for discontinuous change; usually take no action when a client wants cash

⚠️Traps & tips

TrapStock risk premium is (rm − rf)β, not rmβ and not the full required return
TrapInflation shifts the SML in parallel; changing risk aversion changes its slope
TrapThere is no semiweak form of the EMH
TrapExchange fund (diversify, keep value, defer gains) is not an exchange-traded fund
Exam tipFor any CAPM decision, compute the required return first, then compare it with the forecast or the client's hurdle
Exam tipTie EMH questions to the information set: past prices → public → private

🧾Worked example — Should Nora buy this fund? (CAPM decision)

Nora requires 8% from a new equity fund. The fund's beta is 1.1, T-bills yield 3.5%, and the market is expected to return 8.5%.

  1. Market risk premium = 8.5% − 3.5% = 5.0%
  2. Stock risk premium = 5.0% × 1.1 = 5.5%
  3. CAPM expected return = 3.5% + 5.5% = 9.0%
  4. 9.0% ≥ 8% required → acceptable on a risk-adjusted basis

Check: a beta-0.8 fund would give 3.5% + 5.0% × 0.8 = 7.5%, below her 8% requirement → pass.

M1 · 1.1.1
Under which arrangement does the investment banker guarantee the issuer the full proceeds and absorb the loss on any shares it cannot sell?
A. Firm commitment underwriting · B. Best efforts underwriting · C. Private placement · D. Green shoe option
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A — Firm commitment underwriting

In a firm commitment the underwriter buys the entire issue at a set price and resells it, so any shares left unsold are its loss. Under best efforts the banker only tries to sell as much as possible and the company bears the shortfall.

M1 · 1.1.1
Which of these fictional clients qualifies as an accredited investor under Rule 501 of Regulation D?
A. Ivan, net worth of $1.5 million, of which $700,000 is equity in his primary residence · B. Rosa and Tom, joint income of $320,000 in each of the last two years, expecting the same this year · C. Leah, single, income of $185,000 in each of the last two years and expecting a raise · D. A local charity whose total assets are $4 million
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — Rosa and Tom, joint income of $320,000 in each of the last two years, expecting the same this year

Natural persons qualify with joint income above $300,000 (or above $200,000 alone) in each of the two most recent years plus a reasonable expectation of the same (2026 edition figure), or net worth above $1 million excluding the primary residence. Charities need assets above $5 million.

M2 · 2.1.1
Which of these ratings is the LOWEST that still counts as investment grade?
A. BBB− from Standard & Poor's · B. BB+ from Standard & Poor's · C. Ba from Moody's · D. A− from Standard & Poor's
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A — BBB− from Standard & Poor's

Investment grade covers the top four categories: BBB− or better at S&P and Baa or better at Moody's. BB+/Ba and below are high-yield (junk).

M2 · 2.1.1
A bond's yield to maturity climbs from 4.10% to 5.35%. How large was the move in basis points?
A. 12.5 basis points · B. 125 basis points · C. 1.25 basis points · D. 1,250 basis points
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — 125 basis points

A basis point is 1/100 of 1%. Change = 5.35% − 4.10% = 1.25 percentage points × 100 = 125 basis points.

M3 · 3.1.1
From the issuing corporation's point of view, which statement best describes raising capital with common stock instead of bonds?
A. It avoids fixed payments, but dividends are not tax-deductible · B. It avoids fixed payments, and dividends are tax-deductible · C. It requires fixed payments, but dividends are tax-deductible · D. It requires fixed payments, and interest is tax-deductible
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A — It avoids fixed payments, but dividends are not tax-deductible

Equity's corporate advantage is that it needs no fixed payments that could drain operating cash. Its corporate disadvantage is that dividends are not deductible, whereas bond interest is. Because stock lacks a fixed return, equity investors demand a higher expected return than bondholders.

M3 · 3.1.1
Rafael, age 61, plans to retire in two years and fears a recession. He wants to add one stock whose results depend least on the business cycle. Which is the best fit?
A. A national automaker · B. A regional grocery chain · C. A casual-dining restaurant chain · D. An emerging robotics developer
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — A regional grocery chain

Defensive stocks, such as grocery chains, sell everyday necessities, so demand holds up in recessions. They grow slowly but steadily and tend to gain favor in downturns, which fits Rafael's concern.

M4 · 4.1.1
A callable bond is trading above par. Which ordering of its yields is correct, from highest to lowest?
A. YTC, YTM, current yield · B. YTM, current yield, YTC · C. All three are equal · D. Current yield, YTM, YTC
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ D — Current yield, YTM, YTC

For a premium bond, current yield is highest; YTM is lower because it accounts for the premium shrinking to par; YTC is lowest because a call forces that loss sooner.

M4 · 4.1.1
Corinne is looking at a 15-year, $1,000 par bond with a 4% coupon paid semiannually. Comparable bonds now yield 5.5%. What is the bond's intrinsic value?
A. $1,000.00 · B. $1,152.79 · C. $848.13 · D. $849.44
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ C — $848.13

PV of semiannual coupons and par at the market yield. N = 30, I/YR = 5.5 (2 P/YR, so 2.75% per period), PMT = 40 ÷ 2 = 20, FV = 1,000 → PV = $848.13. Market rate above coupon, so a discount price is expected.

M5 · 5.1.1

Which statement best distinguishes asset allocation from diversification?
A. A portfolio can span several asset classes yet still be concentrated in one or two holdings per class. · B. Asset allocation and diversification are two names for the same portfolio process. · C. Diversification is only achieved once a portfolio holds all four broad asset classes. · D. Holding a dozen stocks in varied industries is asset allocation rather than diversification.
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A — A portfolio can span several asset classes yet still be concentrated in one or two holdings per class.

Allocation spreads money among classes (cash, fixed income, equities, real assets); diversification means holding enough loosely correlated securities to remove unsystematic risk. Allocation alone does not guarantee diversification, though a sound allocation usually includes enough securities in each class.

M8 · 8.1.1
Which of the following is one of Markowitz's assumptions underlying modern portfolio theory?

Investors measure portfolio risk with beta instead of standard deviation · D. Investor indifference strengthens the further they look past the holding period
▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ A — Investors base decisions only on expected return and the variability of returns

MPT assumes mean-variance decision making: investors weigh only expected return and risk (standard deviation), prefer more return at a given risk, view each investment as a return distribution over a holding period, and maximize wealth over that period.

M8 · 8.1.1

An optimizer shows that, for the available asset classes, the efficient frontier offers a maximum expected return of 8% at a 12% standard deviation. ▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — The current portfolio is inefficient; the proposed one cannot be built

At 12% standard deviation the best attainable return is 8%. The 6.5% portfolio lies below the frontier (attainable, dominated). The 10% claim lies above the frontier, outside the set of possible portfolios.

▸ Tap to reveal answer
✓ B — Liquidity

Liquidity constraints record money the clients must be able to reach: an emergency reserve and any known outlay within roughly the next three to five years, such as tuition. Many advisors keep money needed inside five years out of the stock market. The return requirement states the target return; laws and regulations mainly concern plans like ERISA trusts; permitted/excluded investments lists the clients’ preferences.
